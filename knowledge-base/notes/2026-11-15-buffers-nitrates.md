# Buffers and nitric-oxide boosters: beta-alanine, citrulline, beetroot nitrate, sodium bicarbonate

Run date: 2026-11-15 · Topic: buffers-nitrates · Studies file: `2026-11-15-buffers-nitrates-studies.csv` (26 rows)

**How to read this.** Each claim carries an evidence strength (strong / moderate / limited / very limited) and a
citation key from the CSV. Access is marked FULL (full text read), ABSTRACT (abstract only) or SECONDARY (seen only
through another source). Acute (same-session) effects are kept apart from longitudinal (training-block) effects.

**Effect-size scale.** An SMD (standardised mean difference) of 0.2 is "small", 0.5 "moderate". In reps-to-failure
terms, an SMD of about 0.2–0.4 in these studies usually means roughly 1–3 extra reps summed across several sets,
not per set.

**The one-line version.** All four give small acute gains in reps to failure, mostly in later sets and longer,
more acidic efforts. None of them improves maximal strength in a way that matters. None has shown a reliable gain in
muscle or long-term strength in trained lifters.

**What was checked.** All 26 DOIs resolve on Crossref. PubMed lists no retraction, erratum or expression of concern
for any of them.

## 1. Beta-alanine (chronic loading; raises muscle carnosine, an intracellular buffer)

### Acute and performance effects (after weeks of loading)

- **The overall effect is real but small, and depends on how long the effort lasts.**
  - Saunders2017 (ABSTRACT) pooled 40 placebo-controlled, double-blind studies (70 measures, n = 1461):
    overall ES 0.18 (95% CI 0.08–0.28).
  - Exercise duration significantly moderated the effect (p = 0.004).
  - Within efforts of 0.5–10 min, "capacity" tests (time to exhaustion) improved (ES 0.50, 0.25–0.75).
    "Performance" tests (fixed-work time trials) did not (ES 0.11, −0.20 to 0.42).
  - Training status did not moderate the effect, and nor did total dose ingested.
  - The SECONDARY claim (via web search) that efforts under about 30–60 s show no benefit traces to an earlier
    meta-analysis (Hobson 2012). I could not open the 2017 full text to confirm the exact short-duration subgroup.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **Trained young men, maximal efforts lasting 0.5–10 min: a small-to-moderate benefit.**
  - Georgiou2024 (ABSTRACT): 18 studies, n = 331. ES 0.39 (0.09–0.69).
  - Efforts of 4–10 min: ES 0.55 (0.07–1.04). Doses of 5.6–6.4 g/day: ES 0.35 (0.09–0.62).
  - These are mostly cycling, rowing and running tests, not lifting sets.
  - **Strength: moderate for that kind of effort; limited for lifting.**
- **Strength and power in resistance-trained people: inconsistent.**
  - Ong2025 (ABSTRACT; a systematic review with no meta-analysis) covered 9 studies, n = 197.
  - Benefits appeared mainly with split doses (for example 0.8 g several times a day, 4–6.4 g/day, for 5–8 weeks),
    and with high-metabolic-stress training: short rests and high volume.
  - Protocols with long rests or low volume often showed nothing. This is narrative synthesis and prone to
    post-hoc pattern-finding.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **"Responders vs non-responders" is not supported.**
  - Esteves2021 (ABSTRACT) was an individual-participant-data meta-analysis of cycling capacity at 110%:
    4 studies with data available out of 6 eligible.
  - Time to exhaustion improved by +7.7 s (95% CrI 1.3–14.3).
  - Change-score SDs were the same with beta-alanine and placebo (15.9 vs 16.1 s). So the scatter in individual
    results is noise, not true responders.
  - Muscle carnosine rose by 18.1 mmol/kg dry muscle (14.5–21.9), and how much it rose did vary between people.
  - **Strength: limited** (one test type, 4 studies).

### Longitudinal (training-block) outcomes

- **Body composition and muscle: no effect.**
  - Ashtary-Larky2022 (ABSTRACT; GRADE-assessed) covered 20 RCTs, n = 492.
  - Fat-free mass WMD +0.05 kg (−0.71 to 0.82), I² = 0%. The result was the same in resistance-training-only
    subgroups and at ≥6 g/day.
  - GRADE certainty was low to moderate.
  - Co-author Stout reports industry-sponsored nutraceutical research and paid talks on beta-alanine.
  - **Strength: moderate** (that there is no meaningful lean-mass effect).
- **Trained lifters, 8 weeks: no extra strength or muscle thickness.**
  - deCamargo2023 (FULL): n = 19 resistance-trained men, average 5.9 years of training. They took 6.4 g/day
    (4 × 1.6 g, CarnoSyn) and trained 4 sessions a week for 8 weeks.
  - Between-group differences in gains:
    - bench 1RM 0.8 kg (p = 0.68);
    - squat 1RM 0.1 kg (p = 0.99);
    - vastus lateralis thickness 1.6 mm (p = 0.31);
    - reps at 60% 1RM, 0.5–0.7 (not significant).
  - No external funding. The study is underpowered: a 1–2 mm difference cannot be ruled out.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Untrained, 10 weeks: no effect on strength, endurance or body composition.**
  - Kendrick2008 (ABSTRACT): n = 26. Carnosine rose by 12.8 mmol/kg dry muscle.
  - **Industry flag:** co-author J.A. Wise is listed with a Natural Alternatives International (NAI, the CarnoSyn
    maker) affiliation (SECONDARY, via a web search of author records).
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **A positive 5-week trial.**
  - Mate-Munoz2018 (ABSTRACT) studied n = 30 strength-trained people doing a leg circuit of 20–40 s work bouts with
    short rests (a very acidic, metabolic protocol).
  - Squat 1RM gain was +24 kg with beta-alanine vs +16 kg with placebo (p = 0.014). Average power at 1RM was +42.6%
    vs +21.1%.
  - It is a small, single trial, the gains are unusually large for 5 weeks, and its training does not look like
    normal hypertrophy work.
  - **Strength: very limited.**

### Dose and side effects

- **Dose.** Trials that work use 3.2–6.4 g/day, split into doses of 0.8–1.6 g (or sustained-release tablets), for at
  least 4 weeks (Georgiou2024, Ong2025, deCamargo2023). Timing around the workout does not matter, because the
  effect comes from muscle carnosine built up over weeks.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **Side effects.** Paraesthesia (tingling) is the only consistently reported side effect: OR 8.9 (95% CrI 2.2–32.6)
  vs placebo (Dolan2019, ABSTRACT; 101 human studies).
  - Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) rose slightly (ES 0.27) but stayed within normal ranges.
  - Dropouts were no higher than on placebo, and there was no change in muscle taurine.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **Industry flag.** NAI holds the beta-alanine patents and has supplied product or funding to many trials.
  - Saunders (lead author of Saunders2017 and author on Esteves2021 and Dolan2019) has publicly disclosed a past
    NAI scholarship and NAI-donated supplements. This is SECONDARY, from his own blog; I could not open the BJSM
    competing-interests text.
  - The Saunders/Gualano group's reviews use rigorous methods. The industry links are a reason for caution, not a
    reason to dismiss them.

## 2. Citrulline (L-citrulline and citrulline malate)

### Acute effects

- **Strength/power across sets: tiny.**
  - Trexler2019 (ABSTRACT) covered 12 studies (13 samples), n = 198: SMD 0.20 (95% CI 0.01–0.39), I² = 0%,
    with no funnel asymmetry.
  - Every individual study's CI crossed zero.
  - **Strength: moderate** (that any effect is small).
- **Reps to failure: about +3 reps in total across about 5 sets.**
  - Varvik2021 (ABSTRACT) covered 8 studies, n = 137, mostly trained, using 6–8 g citrulline malate 40–60 min
    before training.
  - Reps rose by 3 ± 5 out of about 51 total (+6.4%), SMD 0.196 (p = 0.022).
  - Lower body: SMD 0.27 (p = 0.051). Upper body: SMD 0.16 (p = 0.13), not significant.
  - **Strength: limited to moderate.**
- **1RM strength in trained lifters: no effect.**
  - Aguiar2022 (ABSTRACT) covered 4 RCTs: SMD 0.13 (−0.21 to 0.46).
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **The newest and largest synthesis: small and fragile.**
  - Wang2026 (ABSTRACT) was a three-level meta-analysis of 30 RCTs (138 effects, n = 644): g = 0.16 (p = 0.01).
  - Prediction intervals were wide, and GRADE certainty was low to very low.
  - Subgroup "benefits" did not survive sensitivity analyses. The authors call for trials with verified supplement
    composition, because labels on citrulline-malate products are often wrong about the citrulline-to-malate ratio.
  - **Strength: moderate** (that the effect is small and uncertain).
- **Fact-check of the famous "+53% reps" claim.**
  - PerezGuisado2010 (ABSTRACT) gave 8 g citrulline malate to 41 men doing 16 bench sets at 80%. It reported 52.9%
    more reps in the last set and 40% less soreness.
  - It is a single, much larger outlier than every later trial and meta-analysis. The headline number is the final
    set only, not the session total. GI discomfort was reported in 14.6% of participants.
  - Treat it as not reproduced. **Strength: very limited.**
- **A well-run null trial in trained lifters.**
  - Haugen2023 (ABSTRACT; open access, full text not read) had n = 35 resistance-trained men and women take 12 g citrulline malate.
  - Citrulline malate alone had no ergogenic effect on any outcome: 1RM, reps at 60%, or jumps.
  - Caffeine at 5 mg/kg worked: squat reps +18.6%, bench reps +9.3%. Adding citrulline malate to caffeine added
    nothing.
  - **Strength: limited.**

### Longitudinal outcomes

- **6 weeks, n = 33 trained men.**
  - Bayat2025 (ABSTRACT) found a between-group difference only for summed upper-body reps to failure (L-citrulline
    p < 0.001, citrulline malate p = 0.026).
  - There was no difference in 1RM or lower-body reps. No hypertrophy was measured.
  - It was small and tested many outcomes. Co-author Tinsley consults for supplement makers (not this product).
  - **Strength: very limited.**
- **No trial in trained lifters shows citrulline increases muscle growth.**
  - **Strength: none** (no evidence for a benefit).

## 3. Dietary nitrate (beetroot juice)

### Acute effects

- **Squat and bench reps to failure: a small-to-moderate gain, from very few studies.**
  - Tan2023 (FULL) covered 6 studies, n = 92 resistance-trained men.
  - Reps to failure: SMD 0.43 (95% CI 0.16–0.69; I² = 46%; 5 studies). Mean power: SMD 0.40 (0.13–0.68).
  - Peak power: SMD 0.20 (−0.004 to 0.41), not significant. Peak velocity: SMD 0.00.
  - Doses were about 6.4 mmol (one 70 mL shot) or about 13 mmol (two shots), 2–2.5 h before training.
  - Only one study used a properly matched nitrate-depleted beetroot placebo. Almost all used the same commercial
    product (Beet It).
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Broader muscular performance: trivial for strength, small for endurance.**
  - Alvares2022 (ABSTRACT) covered 34 studies: strength SMD 0.08 and endurance SMD 0.31 (p < 0.0001). CIs are not
    given in the abstract.
  - Training level, dose and muscle group did not moderate the effect.
  - **Strength: moderate** (that the strength effect is trivial); **limited to moderate** (endurance).
- **Peak muscle power in a fresh state: about +5%.**
  - Coggan2021 (ABSTRACT) was an individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 19 crossover studies, n = 268.
  - g = 0.42 (0.29–0.56), I² = 23%. A single acute dose did better (0.54) than multi-day dosing (0.22).
  - The tests were mostly isokinetic and cycling power, not barbell lifts.
  - Academic funding (NIH).
  - **Strength: moderate** for power; limited for lifting transfer.

### Longitudinal outcomes

- **12 weeks of resistance training in middle-aged and older adults: no added benefit.**
  - Mueller2026 (ABSTRACT) gave n = 27 adults about 13 mmol/day.
  - Leg strength, strength-endurance and mitochondrial markers showed no extra gain over training alone.
- **Trained young lifters: no hypertrophy data at all.**
- **Strength: very limited / none** (no evidence for a long-term benefit in Kye's population).

### Side effects

- Beetroot juice turns urine and stools pink. That is harmless, but it can look like blood.
- Some people get GI upset.
- **Antibacterial mouthwash blunts the effect**, because the conversion of nitrate to nitrite depends on mouth
  bacteria. (Mechanism: SECONDARY, from the AIS fact sheet and general literature; not re-verified here.)

## 4. Sodium bicarbonate (acute blood buffer)

### Acute effects

- **Muscular endurance improves; strength does not.**
  - Grgic2020 (ABSTRACT) covered 13 endurance studies and 11 strength studies.
  - Muscular endurance: SMD 0.37 (95% CI 0.15–0.59). Small muscle groups: 0.31 (0.04–0.59). Large muscle groups:
    0.40 (0.13–0.66).
  - Strength, rested: SMD 0.02 (−0.09 to 0.13). Strength, fatigued: −0.16 (−0.59 to 0.28).
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **The certainty is lower than the headline suggests.**
  - Grgic2021b (ABSTRACT) is an umbrella review that rated the muscular-endurance evidence low quality, with
    "strongly suspected" publication bias.
  - **Strength: moderate** (that the certainty is low).
- **A trained-lifter RCT shows the effect only appears in later sets.**
  - Varovic2023 (ABSTRACT) had n = 19 trained men take 0.3 g/kg and do 3 sets to failure at 70%.
  - Bench: set-3 reps g = 0.30, total reps g = 0.23. Biceps curl: nothing significant.
  - The benefit appeared only in sets 2–3, which fits a buffering mechanism.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Dose and side effects.**
  - Grgic2021a (FULL; ISSN position stand) gives 0.2 g/kg as the minimum and 0.3 g/kg as the optimum, taken
    60–180 min before exercise.
  - Doses of 0.4–0.5 g/kg add side effects but no benefit.
  - Multi-day loading (0.4–0.5 g/kg/day in split doses for 3–7 days) also works.
  - Common side effects are bloating, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain. Ways to reduce them: a lower dose, taking
    it about 180 min before, taking it with a high-carbohydrate meal, or enteric-coated capsules.
  - The ISSN says part of the effect seems to be placebo.
  - **COI flag:** several authors report supplement-industry grants, advisory roles or royalties (not on
    bicarbonate). Grgic, Saunders and Schoenfeld declare none.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **The sodium cost for Kye.** 0.3 g/kg × 87 kg ≈ 26 g of bicarbonate, which contains about 7.1 g of sodium
  (bicarbonate is 27.4% sodium by mass). That is roughly 3.5 times the Australian suggested dietary target of
  2,000 mg/day, in one go.

### Longitudinal outcomes

- **No adaptation benefit in resistance training.**
  - Siegler2018 (ABSTRACT; PLoS One) had n = 8 trained people take 0.3 g/kg before every session for
    10 weeks of leg-extension training.
  - Bicarbonate reduced acute fatigue in rate of torque development by about 20% in one session. But it gave no
    extra 1RM, maximal torque or rep gains over 10 weeks.
  - Very small sample.
  - **Strength: very limited.**

## 5. Combined buffers and a striking hypertrophy claim

- **Pouresmaeili2026 (ABSTRACT)** gave n = 20 "active" men (10 per group) beta-alanine (3–6 g/day) plus bicarbonate
  (4–6 g/day) for 8 weeks of 3 × max-rep sets at 70%.
  - It reported total session volume +29% vs +13%, and MRI quadriceps cross-sectional area **+21–24% vs +5%**.
  - 1RM gains did not differ.
- **Fact-check verdict: do not trust the hypertrophy number.**
  - A 4-fold difference in muscle growth from buffers is far outside anything else in this literature; for
    comparison, beta-alanine's fat-free-mass effect in Ashtary-Larky2022 is 0.05 kg.
  - The abstract mixes mm³ and mm² for the same measure.
  - The groups are tiny and the participants untrained. The bicarbonate dose is low (about 0.05–0.07 g/kg), so it
    is unlikely to buffer much.
  - Only the abstract was accessible: blinding details were not visible.
  - **Strength: very limited.** Needs replication before it means anything.
- Saunders2017 found beta-alanine plus bicarbonate gave the largest effect vs placebo (ES 0.43, 0.22–0.64) in
  endurance-type capacity tests, not lifting.

## 6. Does any of this matter for 6–12-rep hypertrophy sets?

- **Mechanism vs set length.**
  - A 6–12-rep set lasts about 20–50 s. Buffering mostly helps efforts of about 30 s to 10 min, and the benefit
    grows over repeated sets with incomplete recovery (Saunders2017, Grgic2021a, Varovic2023).
  - So a buffer can add a rep or so to the 3rd or 4th set of an exercise with short rest. It does little for a
    single heavy set, or for sets with 2–3 min rest.
  - **Strength: limited** (consistent across studies, but inferred).
- **Reps are not muscle.**
  - The extra reps happen at failure, which already gives near-maximal stimulus.
  - The longitudinal trials in trained lifters (deCamargo2023, Siegler2018) and the body-composition meta-analysis
    (Ashtary-Larky2022) show no translation into more muscle or strength.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Longer efforts.**
  - Sets of 15–30+ reps, giant sets, circuits, 1–5 min conditioning, rowing or sled work are where beta-alanine and
    bicarbonate actually earn their place (Georgiou2024, Grgic2020).
  - **Strength: moderate.**

## Verdict

- **Beta-alanine.** It really works for sustained high-intensity efforts of 0.5–10 min. For classic hypertrophy
  training it gives at most a small extra-reps effect in later sets with short rests, and no proven extra muscle or
  strength in trained lifters. It is safe, apart from harmless tingling. It is AIS Group A, but for endurance-type
  high-intensity work.
- **Citrulline malate.** The weakest of the four. The pooled effect is about SMD 0.16–0.20, roughly +3 reps across a
  whole 5-set exercise. Good trials in trained lifters (Haugen2023) find nothing, and the famous +53% result has
  never been reproduced. Products are often mislabelled. It is not in AIS Group A (its exact AIS group was not
  verified this run).
- **Beetroot nitrate.** Small acute gains in reps and bar power: SMD about 0.4 for squat and bench, but from only 6
  small studies, nearly all using one product. There are no longitudinal hypertrophy data in trained lifters. It is
  AIS Group A, mainly on endurance evidence.
- **Sodium bicarbonate.** The most reliable acute rep booster (SMD about 0.37), though evidence certainty is low.
  It does nothing for strength. The GI risk is real, and the 7 g sodium hit at Kye's weight is large. It is
  impractical for daily 1.5 h sessions, and there is no added adaptation over 10 weeks. It is AIS Group A, for
  events lasting 1–7 min.
- **Overall.** These are performance-of-the-day tools for acidic efforts, not muscle builders. Next to creatine
  (an adaptation effect) and caffeine (a reliable acute effect), all four are second-tier for Kye's goals.

## Practical recommendation for Kye

1. **Skip citrulline malate.** It is cheap to buy but the evidence is poor. If your pre-workout already contains it,
   the 6–8 g in it is harmless; just don't pay extra for it.
2. **Beta-alanine is optional. Use it only if your training has a lot of short-rest, high-rep or density work**:
   giant sets, drop sets, 15+ rep work, conditioning finishers.
   - If you use it, take 3.2–6.4 g/day (for example 4 × 0.8–1.6 g with meals, or sustained-release tablets) every
     day for at least 4 weeks. Timing around the workout doesn't matter.
   - Expect tingling with doses above about 1.6 g.
   - For straight 6–12-rep sets with 2–3 min rest, don't expect to notice anything.
3. **Beetroot juice is optional and cheap to test.**
   - Take one or two 70 mL concentrated shots (about 6–13 mmol nitrate) 2–3 h before a session you care about.
     Don't use antibacterial mouthwash.
   - Expect at most about 1 extra rep across several sets, plus pink urine.
   - Don't bother taking it daily for "gains": there are no longitudinal data showing extra muscle.
4. **Don't use sodium bicarbonate for routine training.**
   - At 0.3 g/kg that's about 26 g of bicarbonate and about 7 g of sodium, 1–3 h before training, with a real chance
     of GI distress.
   - It makes sense only for a specific test or event lasting 30 s–10 min (for example a rowing test or a CrossFit
     workout). Trial it in practice first, with a carb meal, or as enteric-coated capsules.
   - Avoid it if you have high blood pressure or kidney issues.
5. **Priorities.** Creatine monohydrate daily, plus caffeine on hard days, plus training to near failure, deliver far
   more than any of these four. If you buy one of the four, beta-alanine is the most defensible, but only for
   high-rep, short-rest work.
6. **Supplement safety (Australian context).** Prefer batch-tested products (for example HASTA or Informed Sport) if
   drug testing is ever relevant. Single-ingredient powders let you control dose; pre-workouts often under-dose
   beta-alanine and citrulline.

## New avenues

1. Do beta-alanine or bicarbonate increase **total session volume** in trained lifters doing short-rest
   hypertrophy programmes? And does that extra volume produce measurable extra hypertrophy over 8–12 weeks? This
   needs a replication of the Pouresmaeili2026 claim with proper blinding and power.
2. How much do **pre-workout products actually contain** versus their labels, for citrulline-to-malate ratio and
   beta-alanine dose? Are any Australian products batch-tested?
3. Does **enteric-coated or multi-day-loaded bicarbonate** remove the GI problem enough to make it practical for
   high-rep leg sessions, and what is the blood-pressure or sodium cost of using it regularly?
4. Is there evidence that **nitrate's acute power effect** (Coggan2021: +5% fresh-state power) improves velocity-based
   or explosive resistance training outcomes over weeks in trained lifters?
