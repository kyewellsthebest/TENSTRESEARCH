# Protein powders: whey vs casein vs plant, leucine threshold, digestibility, contamination testing

Run date: 2026-11-15 · Topic: protein-powders · Studies file: `2026-11-15-protein-powders-studies.csv` (21 rows)

**How to read this.** Each claim carries an evidence strength (strong / moderate / limited / very limited) and a
citation key from the CSV. Access is marked FULL (full text read), ABSTRACT (abstract only) or SECONDARY (seen only
through another source, such as news coverage or a review). Acute muscle protein synthesis (MPS) studies, which
measure a few hours of signalling, are kept apart from longitudinal training studies, which measure actual muscle
and strength gained over weeks or months. An acute MPS difference does not on its own show a long-term difference.

**The bottom line in one sentence.** Total daily protein is the main thing. Once that is high enough (about
1.6 g/kg/day, possibly up to 2.2), the source of the powder (whey, casein, soy, pea or rice) makes little or no
measurable difference to muscle or strength in young trained men. The real differences between powders are price,
taste, digestion, and how much lead and cadmium they contain.

---

## 1. Does a protein powder add anything beyond total daily protein?

- **Protein supplementation adds a small amount of muscle and strength to training.** Morton2018 (FULL) pooled
  49 RCTs (n = 1863) of training for at least 6 weeks:
  - fat-free mass +0.30 kg (95% CI 0.09–0.52), with I² = 7%;
  - 1RM +2.49 kg (0.64–4.33);
  - muscle fibre CSA +310 µm² (51–570);
  - **Strength: strong.**
- **The benefit was larger in trained lifters, but those numbers are less precise.**
  - Trained subgroup: fat-free mass +1.05 kg (0.61–1.50) and 1RM +4.27 kg (0.61–7.94).
  - Only 10 of the 49 studies used trained participants. In the meta-regression, training status added 0.75 kg
    (0.09–1.40).
  - **Strength: moderate** (few studies, wide CIs).
- **Most of the benefit is explained by total protein intake, not by the powder itself.**
  - Morton2018's break-point analysis found no further fat-free-mass gain above 1.62 g/kg/day (95% CI 1.03–2.20).
  - That break point was not statistically significant (p = 0.079; 42 study arms; R² = 0.19).
  - The authors' own reading is that ~2.2 g/kg/day, the upper CI, is a sensible ceiling for people who want to make
    sure they reach it.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **No trial shows that a powder beats the same amount of protein from food.** The supplementation trials compare
  protein with a placebo or a lower total intake, not powder with protein-matched whole food.
  - Hevia-Larraín2021 matched total protein at 1.6 g/kg/day using either soy or whey powder on top of each diet. Both
    groups gained the same.
  - **Strength: moderate** that powder is only a convenient way to hit the total. I found no direct powder-vs-food
    RCT in trained lifters, so a small timing or format effect cannot be excluded.
- **Conflict-of-interest flags (Morton2018).**
  - Phillips (senior author) has received grants, travel and honoraria from the US National Dairy Council, which
    funded some of the trials pooled.
  - A 2020 correction (10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608corr1, FULL) added a disclosure that Schoenfeld sat on the
    advisory board of Dymatize Nutrition, a supplement maker.
  - There is no retraction, and neither issue changes the data.

## 2. Whey vs casein vs plant proteins, with total protein matched (longitudinal outcomes)

### Meta-analyses
- **Soy vs whey or other animal protein: no difference in strength or lean mass.**
  - Messina2018 (ABSTRACT): 9 RCTs, n = 266. Bench press p = 0.88–0.90, squat p = 0.38–0.64, lean mass p = 0.80–0.96.
    Results are reported only as χ² heterogeneity tests, with no pooled effect size or CI in the abstract.
  - Lead author Mark Messina is Executive Director of the Soy Nutrition Institute, funded via the United Soybean
    Board (SECONDARY). I could not read the paper's own COI statement.
  - The pooled studies are mostly in untrained people.
  - **Strength: moderate** for "no large difference"; non-significance here is not proof the two are equal.
- **Animal vs plant protein: absolute lean mass slightly favours animal protein, but not significantly. Strength
  does not differ.**
  - Lim2021 (FULL), 16 RCTs: absolute lean mass +0.22 kg for animal protein (95% CI −0.02 to 0.46); percent lean mass
    +0.50% (0.05–0.95).
  - In adults under 50: +0.41 kg (0.08–0.74).
  - 1RM squat WMD −0.94 kg (−4.57 to 2.70), which is no difference.
  - No industry funding.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **The largest and most recent pooling finds a small edge for animal protein. The edge is driven by non-soy plant
  proteins and whole plant-based diets, not by soy.**
  - Reid-McCann2025 (FULL): 43 RCTs, of which 30 were meta-analysed. Muscle mass SMD −0.20 for plant protein (95% CI
    −0.37 to −0.03).
  - **Soy vs milk: SMD −0.02 (−0.20 to 0.16; 17 RCTs), which is no difference.**
  - Non-soy plant proteins (rice, chia, oat, potato) vs animal: SMD −0.58 (−1.06 to −0.09; only 5 RCTs).
  - Plant-based diets vs animal diets: SMD −0.51 (−0.91 to −0.11).
  - With resistance training: SMD −0.45 (−0.85 to −0.05).
  - No strength difference.
  - No industry funding.
  - **Strength: moderate** for "soy ≈ dairy". **Limited** for "rice or pea is worse", which rests on 5 small trials.
- **Long-term (6 months or more): no difference.**
  - Yimam2026 (FULL): 18 RCTs, n = 1893, mostly soy and mostly older adults. Lean body mass SMD 0.26 (−0.58 to 0.9 as
    printed), with I² = 92%.
  - Flag: Danone paid the article fee, co-authors are at the Danone Research & Innovation Center, and the lead author
    did the work during a Danone internship. Danone sells both dairy and plant proteins.
  - **Strength: limited** for lifters (wrong population, huge heterogeneity).

### Key trials
- **Vegan + soy vs omnivore + whey, total protein matched at 1.6 g/kg/day: identical gains.**
  - Hevia-Larraín2021 (ABSTRACT): n = 38 untrained young men, 12 weeks.
  - Leg lean mass +1.2 ± 1.0 kg (vegan) vs +1.2 ± 0.8 kg (omnivore).
  - Vastus lateralis CSA +2.2 vs +2.8 cm².
  - Leg-press 1RM +97 vs +117 kg.
  - No between-group differences. Funded by FAPESP (public).
  - Limitations: untrained participants, small n (so a modest difference could be missed), and the trial was
    retrospectively registered.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **Whey vs soy at a lower total intake (1.4 g/kg/day): whey won.**
  - Volek2013 (ABSTRACT): n = 63 untrained adults, about 9 months.
  - Lean body mass +3.3 ± 1.5 kg (whey) vs +1.8 ± 1.6 kg (soy) vs +2.3 ± 1.7 kg (carbohydrate).
  - Funded by the Dairy Research Institute, the US dairy checkoff (SECONDARY, via trade press).
  - It is odd that soy did worse than carbohydrate, which suggests noise.
  - **Strength: limited.** This is the main trial behind "whey > soy", and it fits a "source matters when total
    protein is marginal" reading.
- **Rice vs whey in trained men: no difference, but the trials were funded by the rice-protein company.**
  - Joy2013 (FULL): n = 24 resistance-trained men, 48 g of rice or whey isolate on training days, 8 weeks. Lean body
    mass rose in both (rice 58.5→61.0 kg, whey 59.6→62.8 kg) with no group × time interaction.
    - Funded by Increnovo LLC, a consultancy whose staff were co-authors, using Axiom's Oryzatein rice protein.
  - Moon2020 (FULL): n = 24 trained men, 24 g/day, 8 weeks. Between-group 95% CIs: fat-free mass −0.68 to 1.99 kg,
    bench 1RM −13.8 to 7.1 kg.
    - Funded by Axiom Foods (unrestricted grant).
  - Both trials are tiny, so a real difference of 1–2 kg lean mass could be missed.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Pea vs whey: no difference between the two protein groups.**
  - Babault2015 (FULL): n = 161 randomised, 137 analysed. Moderately active men (2–6 h of activity a week, not
    lifters). 2 × 25 g/day for 12 weeks.
  - Biceps thickness differed between groups only as a trend (p = 0.09). Pea beat placebo only in a post-hoc
    "weakest participants" subgroup. Whey was not different from either.
  - Funded by Roquette, the maker of NUTRALYS pea protein, and three authors were Roquette employees.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Whey vs casein: no difference.**
  - Wilborn2013 (ABSTRACT): n = 16 female college basketball players, 24 g before and after training,
    8 weeks.
  - Lean mass +1.5 ± 1.0 kg (whey) vs +1.4 ± 1.0 kg (casein). No interaction for any variable.
  - Far too small to detect anything modest.
  - **Strength: very limited.** No adequately powered whey-vs-casein trial in trained men was found.

## 3. The leucine threshold: acute MPS vs long-term outcomes

**Acute physiology (hours).**
- **Whey stimulates MPS more than casein, and somewhat more than soy, in the first hours after training.**
  - Tang2009 (ABSTRACT): n = 6 per group, each given 10 g of essential amino acids. After exercise, whey MPS was
    ~122% greater than casein and ~31% greater than soy.
  - Very small groups, and the measure was mixed MPS rather than myofibrillar MPS.
  - **Strength: moderate** for the acute effect.
- **Leucine is a trigger, but the rest of the protein also matters.**
  - Churchward-Venne2012 (ABSTRACT): 6.25 g whey topped up with leucine matched 25 g whey for MPS at 1–3 h, but only
    the full 25 g whey kept MPS elevated at 3–5 h after exercise (+184% vs +55%).
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **More leucine raised day-long (integrated) MPS in older women.**
  - Devries2018 (ABSTRACT): 15 g protein with 4.2 g leucine vs 1.3 g leucine, n = 11 per group. Integrated
    myofibrillar MPS in the exercised leg rose 17% vs 7%.
  - Wrong population for Kye. Funded by PepsiCo: two authors were from PepsiCo R&D.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **A good plant blend can match dairy acutely.**
  - Pinckaers2023 (FULL): 30 g of a wheat + corn + pea blend vs 30 g milk protein, n = 24 young men. Myofibrillar MPS
    was 0.064 vs 0.053 %/h, p = 0.08, numerically favouring the blend. Milk raised plasma essential amino acids about
    twice as much.
  - Funded by Tereos, Cargill and Kellogg via a public–private partnership (TiFN).
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Pooled acute data show at most a trivial edge for animal protein in young adults.**
  - Mendes2026 (ABSTRACT): 12 studies, Bayesian meta-analysis. Effect size for plant vs animal 0.004 %/h (95% CrI
    −0.002 to 0.011).
  - Young adults showed similar responses. Over-65s showed a modest animal advantage: 0.013 (0.000–0.027).
  - **Strength: moderate.**

**Long-term (weeks to months).**
- **The acute whey advantage largely disappears in training studies once total protein is adequate.**
  - See section 2: Messina2018, Lim2021, Reid-McCann2025 (soy vs milk), Hevia-Larraín2021 and Moon2020.
  - The "leucine threshold" (often quoted as ~2–3 g per meal; I did not verify a specific source for that number this
    run) is real acute physiology. But eating enough total protein, spread over several meals, appears to cover it.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
  - Where it may still matter: low total protein, low per-meal doses, older lifters, or poorly formulated single-source
    plant powders such as rice or hemp alone.

## 4. Digestibility and protein quality (DIAAS / PDCAAS)

DIAAS ranks a protein by its most limiting digestible essential amino acid. A score of 100 or more is "excellent",
75–99 is "high", and below 75 earns no quality claim.

- **Quality ranking.** Herreman2020 (FULL) pooled pig ileal digestibility data using the 0.5–3-year reference
  pattern:
  - casein, egg, pork and potato scored ≥100;
  - whey and soy scored 75–99;
  - pea, rice, hemp, oat, wheat and corn scored below 75.
  - Blends (for example potato, soy or pea with cereals) raise DIAAS.
  - Whey products ranged from 78 to 88 depending on processing.
  - Flag: all four authors are employees of Avebe, a potato-protein company, and potato came out on top.
  - **Strength: moderate** for the ranking. The scores shift with the reference pattern and with processing.
- **Amino acid content.** Gorissen2018 (FULL) measured commercial isolates:
  - essential amino acids made up 43% of whey, 39% of milk protein and 34% of casein, against 21–22% of oat, lupin
    and wheat;
  - leucine ranged from 5.1% (hemp) to 13.5% (corn);
  - lysine and methionine were lower in plant proteins (about 3.6% and 1.0%, vs 7.0% and 2.5% in animal proteins).
  - Funded by a public–private partnership. Senior authors have industry honoraria.
  - **Strength: strong** for "plant isolates differ a lot; blends fix most of it".
- **What this means in practice.** The DIAAS gap is real, but can be covered by a slightly bigger serve of plant
  protein (about 10–25% more), a blend such as pea + rice, or soy. Training trials (section 2) show the gap does not
  turn into measurably less muscle once total protein is adequate, at least for soy.
  - **Strength: moderate.**

## 5. Contamination: heavy metals, label accuracy, amino spiking, banned substances

- **Plant powders carry more lead and cadmium than whey.** This is consistent across sources.
  - Clean Label Project 2025 (SECONDARY, via news coverage; not peer-reviewed): 160 products. About 77% of plant
    powders vs 28% of whey powders exceeded Prop 65 lead limits. Chocolate flavours had about 4× the lead of vanilla.
  - Consumer Reports, October 2025 (SECONDARY; not peer-reviewed): 23 products. Plant products averaged about 9× the
    lead of dairy products. Two plant products were "avoid".
  - Both use California Prop 65 (0.5 µg lead/day), which is a very conservative benchmark.
  - Industry bodies (the Natural Products Association) disputed the framing.
  - **Strength: moderate** for "plant > whey for heavy metals". **Limited** for the size of any health risk.
- **Peer-reviewed risk modelling says typical use is low risk, but this comes with caveats.**
  - Bandara2020 (FULL) re-analysed the Consumer Reports and Clean Label Project data. All hazard indices were below 1
    except one product at 3 servings/day (13.18 µg Cd/serving). Modelled blood lead levels stayed under the CDC's
    5 µg/dL even at 3 servings/day (max 2.35 µg/dL in one gainer).
  - Caveats:
    - The authors work at Cardno ChemRisk, a consultancy that often does industry-side work. They declared no COI.
    - CDC lowered its reference to 3.5 µg/dL in 2021, after this paper.
    - Lead has no known safe threshold.
  - Horváth2025 (FULL): 22 products in Hungary. None exceeded regulatory limits.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Label fraud and amino spiking are real, but well documented only in weakly regulated markets.**
  - Philips2024 (FULL, self-funded): 36 Indian-market products. 25 (69.4%) contained less protein than labelled, some
    by more than 50%. Some contained more than labelled, which suggests spiking with glycine or taurine. 13.9% had
    aflatoxins, and 8.3% had pesticide residues.
  - Multinational brands performed better.
  - **Strength: limited** for Australia (different market, small sample).
- **Banned-substance risk in Australia is low for plain protein powder, but not zero.**
  - Barker2025 (FULL): Sport Integrity Australia / HASTA bought 200 products online in 2022 (201 samples, as one product was tested as two capsules) [corrected in audit]. 35% contained
    WADA-prohibited substances, mostly in pre-workouts, fat burners and "muscle builders". Protein powders: 1 of 14
    (7%). 57% of the positive products did not list the substance on the label.
  - **Strength: moderate.** This matters only if Kye is ever drug-tested in competition.

## Verdict

- **Protein source barely matters for a well-fed lifter. Total protein matters far more.**
  - With total intake at about 1.6 g/kg/day or more, soy ≈ whey for muscle and strength. Soy vs milk SMD −0.02
    (−0.20 to 0.16) in Reid-McCann2025.
  - Hevia-Larraín2021 showed identical gains with vegan + soy vs omnivore + whey.
- **Whey is still the best default per gram**: highest essential amino acids and leucine, fastest MPS response,
  lowest heavy metals, cheap in Australia. But the edge on actual muscle gain is small to undetectable.
- **Casein vs whey**: no longitudinal evidence of a difference in useful trials. Casein's only real niche is as a slow
  pre-sleep option, which belongs to the separate protein-timing topic.
- **Single-source rice, pea or hemp powders**: probably fine at large serves, but the evidence base is small and
  industry-funded, and the pooled data hint at slightly worse outcomes than animal or soy protein (limited evidence).
  They are also the products most likely to carry lead and cadmium.
- **"Leucine threshold"**: real in acute studies, mostly irrelevant long-term if every meal has a decent serve of
  protein.
- **Does powder add anything?** Only convenience and cost. It is a food, not an ergogenic aid.

## Practical recommendation for Kye

1. **Hit the total first:** about 140 g/day (1.6 g/kg × 87 kg) as a working minimum. Up to about 190 g/day
   (2.2 g/kg) is a reasonable ceiling if he is cutting or wants a safety margin. Powder is only worth using if it
   makes reaching that easier or cheaper.
2. **Default pick:** a whey protein concentrate or isolate (WPC is fine unless lactose bothers him). Use 30–40 g of
   protein per serve, which reliably gives a leucine-rich dose. There is no need for hydrolysates, "anabolic" blends,
   added BCAAs or casein-only products.
3. **If he wants plant protein:** choose soy isolate, or a pea + rice blend, and add about 10–20% more per serve.
   Prefer unflavoured or vanilla over chocolate (chocolate carries more lead and cadmium), and pick brands that
   publish heavy-metal batch results.
4. **Australian buying rules:**
   - Buy from established brands sold through Australian retailers.
   - Avoid cheap grey imports and "proprietary blend" or added-amino-acid labels, which are spiking red flags.
   - If he ever competes in a tested federation, use only products with HASTA or Informed Sport batch certification,
     and check the batch number in the Sport Integrity app.
   - Third-party testing covers banned substances and is a reasonable quality signal. It does not, by itself,
     certify heavy-metal levels or protein content.
5. **Do not pay more for marketing claims.** "Grass-fed", "leucine-boosted" or "fast-absorbing" have no outcome data
   behind them for trained lifters.

## Doubts and gaps

- Almost all longitudinal head-to-head trials are in untrained people or are tiny. Trained-lifter trials (Joy2013,
  Moon2020) are funded by the rice-protein company.
- Messina2018 reports only p-values in the abstract, and I could not read its funding statement.
- Volek2013's funding is confirmed only through trade press.
- The Clean Label Project and Consumer Reports numbers are SECONDARY (news coverage), not peer-reviewed.
- I found no Australian peer-reviewed heavy-metal survey of protein powders.
- Hevia-Larraín2021, Tang2009, Volek2013, Devries2018, Churchward-Venne2012 and Mendes2026 were read as abstracts only.

## New avenues

1. **Heavy metals in protein powders sold in Australia.** Is there any Australian peer-reviewed or FSANZ/NMI survey
   of lead and cadmium in whey and plant powders? How do they compare with FSANZ limits?
2. **Whey vs casein vs blend for pre-sleep protein in trained lifters.** Do longitudinal trials show a difference in
   lean mass or 1RM when total daily protein is matched?
3. **Protein powder vs whole-food protein, matched for total intake.** Are there RCTs showing any difference in body
   composition, appetite or adherence during a cut in trained lifters?
4. **Collagen and "protein-spiked" collagen blends.** Does collagen count towards hypertrophy protein targets? Recent
   RCTs suggest it may not (to be verified).

**140 g/day check.** 1.6 × 87 = 139 g, so "about 140 g/day" is right, and 2.2 × 87 = 191 g, so "about 190 g" is also
right. The framing is sensible, with one nuance. Morton's 1.62 g/kg is a **population-average plateau with a wide,
non-significant CI (1.03–2.20)**, not a proven individual minimum. "Target about 140 g; up to about 190 g if you want to
be sure" is a slightly more accurate reading than "working minimum". No change is needed to the advice itself.

**Safety.** The note gives no banned-substance dosing or sourcing, and its batch-testing advice is appropriate.

**Verdict stands.**
