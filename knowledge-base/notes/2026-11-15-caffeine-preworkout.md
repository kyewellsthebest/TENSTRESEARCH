# Caffeine and pre-workouts: performance, dose by body weight, sleep cost, habituation

Run date: 2026-11-15 · Topic: caffeine-preworkout · Studies file: `2026-11-15-caffeine-preworkout-studies.csv` (20 rows)

**How to read this.** Each claim carries an evidence strength (strong / moderate / limited / very limited) and a
citation key from the CSV. Access is marked FULL (full text read), ABSTRACT (abstract only) or SECONDARY (seen only
through another source). Acute session performance is kept separate from long-term adaptation throughout.

**Effect-size scale.** SMD (standardised mean difference) of about 0.2 is "small". In a trained lifter, an SMD of
0.2 on 1RM works out to roughly 1–2% of the lift.

---

## 1. Acute effects on strength, power, reps to failure and volume

- **Maximal strength (1RM) goes up a little.**
  - Grgic2018 (FULL): SMD 0.20 (95% CI 0.03–0.36) across 10 studies. Upper-body strength rose (SMD 0.21, 0.02–0.39);
    lower-body strength did not reach significance (SMD 0.15, −0.05 to 0.34).
  - Heterogeneity was I² = 0%, and the funnel plot showed no clear asymmetry.
  - Only 3 included studies checked whether blinding worked. In those, 33–60% of participants correctly guessed
    which trial was caffeine.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **Power and bar velocity improve, by a small-to-moderate amount.**
  - Grgic2018: jump power SMD 0.17 (0.00–0.34).
  - Xiao2025 (FULL): 12 studies, n = 230. Mean bar velocity during resistance exercise SMD 0.42 (0.19–0.65), but with
    I² = 85%. Mean power SMD 0.21 (0.12–0.30), I² = 14%. Velocity improved at light, moderate and heavy loads.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **Muscular endurance (reps to failure) shows the most consistent gain, but it is still small.**
  - Bilondi2024 (FULL) pooled 9 meta-analyses: endurance SMD 0.30 (0.21–0.38) and strength SMD 0.18 (0.14–0.21).
    These meta-analyses share many of the same primary studies, so the very narrow CIs overstate how precise the
    estimate really is.
  - A corrigendum exists for Bilondi2024. Its PMC text points to an unrelated article, so I could not see what was
    corrected.
  - Ferreira2021 (ABSTRACT; doi 10.1080/10408398.2020.1781051): about +0.9 bench-press reps (WMD 0.87, 95% CI
    0.33–1.41; 15 studies) and +2.0 kg on bench 1RM (0.20–3.80; 7 studies). Leg-press reps (+1.24, −0.21 to 2.70)
    and leg-press 1RM were not significant.
  - Grgic2020, an umbrella review of 21 meta-analyses (ABSTRACT; full text blocked here), rated the evidence for
    endurance, strength and anaerobic power as moderate quality. It also notes that the 95% prediction intervals
    did not always exclude zero, so an individual's response is uncertain.
  - **Endurance: moderate. "≈1 extra rep per set at moderate loads": moderate for bench, limited for legs.**
- **Training volume in a real session is not proven.**
  - Filip-Stachnik2023 (ABSTRACT): 10 resistance-trained men already habituated to caffeine did a full-body session to
    failure after 3 mg/kg. Total reps were 185 with caffeine vs 180 with placebo (p = 0.28, ES 0.34, not
    significant). Time under tension and RPE did not differ.
  - Most positive data come from single-set tests, not whole sessions of 1.5 h.
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **Industry ties.** The authors of the ISSN position stand (Guest2021, FULL) declare advisory roles with supplement
  companies, a coffee-company consultancy and a patent. The ISSN itself has received grants from companies that sell
  caffeine products. The meta-analytic results above come from independent groups and still agree, so this is a flag,
  not a reason to discard the position stand.

## 2. Dose by body weight, including low doses

- **The standard effective range is 3–6 mg/kg, taken about 60 min before training.** Guest2021 (FULL) adds that
  "minimal effective doses ... may be as low as 2 mg/kg". **Strength: moderate.**
- **Doses of 1–2 mg/kg still work for resistance exercise.**
  - Grgic2022 (ABSTRACT): 12 studies using 0.9–2 mg/kg.
    - strength d 0.17 (0.03–0.31);
    - endurance d 0.21 (0.07–0.35);
    - mean velocity d 0.56 (0.12–1.01).
  - These effects are about the same size as those at higher doses. Note that this is a single-author review.
  - **Strength: limited–moderate.**
- **Higher doses help heavy single reps a little more but cost much more in side effects.**
  - Pallarés2013 (ABSTRACT): 13 resistance-trained men. 3 mg/kg improved velocity at light loads (25–50% 1RM).
  - A dose of 9 mg/kg was needed to improve bench velocity and squat power at 90% 1RM.
  - At 9 mg/kg, adverse effects rose sharply (reported by 15–62% of participants).
  - Xiao2025's dose subgroups for velocity overlap: low (≤3 mg/kg) SMD 0.43, moderate 0.31, high (>6 mg/kg) 0.78.
    The difference between doses was not significant.
  - Guest2021: about 9 mg/kg "do[es] not seem to be required".
  - **Strength: limited.**
- **For Kye at 87 kg:**

  | dose | caffeine |
  |---|---|
  | 1 mg/kg | ≈ 87 mg |
  | 2 mg/kg | ≈ 175 mg |
  | 3 mg/kg | ≈ 260 mg |
  | 6 mg/kg | ≈ 520 mg |

  - EFSA's 2015 opinion (doi 10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102; SECONDARY, opinion not read in full this run) says that for
    healthy non-pregnant adults, single doses up to about 200 mg (about 3 mg/kg) and up to about 400 mg/day raise no
    safety concerns. FSANZ uses the same ballpark in Australia.
  - So 6 mg/kg for Kye (about 520 mg) is above the daily guidance from one scoop alone.

## 3. Sleep cost of training-time caffeine

- **Caffeine reliably harms the night's sleep.**
  - Gardiner2023 (ABSTRACT), 24 studies:
    - total sleep time −45 min;
    - sleep efficiency −7%;
    - time to fall asleep +9 min;
    - time awake after falling asleep +12 min;
    - deep sleep (N3/N4) −11.4 min.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **The "13 hours" claim is a model estimate, not a measurement.** Gardiner2023 models that coffee (107 mg) should be
  taken at least 8.8 h before bed, and a standard pre-workout serve (217.5 mg) at least 13.2 h before bed. These
  cut-offs are extrapolated from a meta-regression. No trial measured them directly. Treat them as order-of-magnitude
  guidance: **limited** as precise cut-offs.
- **The direct trial evidence points the same way.** Drake2013 (ABSTRACT): 400 mg taken 0, 3 or 6 h before bed
  disturbed sleep at all three times, including 6 h. Its conclusion: "caffeine taken 6 hours before bedtime has
  important disruptive effects". **Strength: limited (small home study, single high dose).**
- **Athletes who train in the evening show the same pattern.**
  - Kocak2025 (FULL): 10 studies, 128 athletes, 3–6 mg/kg before late-afternoon or evening sessions.
  - Sleep efficiency −4.9% (95% CI −7.5 to −2.3). This result did not hold up in leave-one-out sensitivity analysis.
  - Total sleep time −32 min (−70 to +5), not significant.
  - Subjective sleep was consistently much worse.
  - In one included triathlete RCT (2 × 3 mg/kg around a 17:00–19:00 session), total sleep time dropped from about
    464 to about 391 min and REM sleep fell.
  - Authors' funding came from an Australian Government scholarship, not industry.
  - **Strength: limited (small, mostly male).**
- **Bottom line for sleep.** For someone training hard 1.5 h a day, losing 30–45 min of sleep and some deep sleep is
  a real cost, and it plausibly outweighs a 1–2% strength bump. Whether that lost sleep harms muscle growth is a
  separate topic in the queue ("Sleep and muscle").

## 4. Habituation and tolerance in habitual users

- **Regular coffee drinkers still get an ergogenic effect.**
  - Grgic&Mikulic2021 (ABSTRACT): 24 resistance-trained men. Low users (about 65 mg/day) and moderate-to-high users
    (about 235 mg/day) benefited equally from 3 mg/kg: +1.2 reps, +0.02–0.08 m/s bar velocity. Habitual intake
    showed no interaction.
  - Gonçalves2017 (ABSTRACT; doi 10.1152/japplphysiol.00260.2017; not in CSV) found the same in 40 cyclists.
  - **Strength: moderate.**
- **The effect may be smaller in heavy users.**
  - Xiao2025 subgroup: the velocity effect was larger in people habitually taking <3 mg/kg/day (SMD 0.87,
    0.72–1.02) than in those taking ≥3 mg/kg/day (SMD 0.21). The reported CI for the second group, 0.11–1.02, looks
    like a typo.
  - Wilk2019 (ABSTRACT): 15 strength-trained men averaging 426 mg/day saw no significant change in bench velocity or
    power at 3, 6 or 9 mg/kg. Large effect sizes appeared only at 6–9 mg/kg.
  - Filip-Stachnik2023: habituated men did not gain significant volume (see section 1).
  - **Strength: limited.** The honest picture is "probably still works, probably less".
- **Daily dosing builds partial tolerance within about 2–3 weeks.**
  - Lara2019 (FULL): 11 participants took 3 mg/kg every day for 20 days.
  - Peak cycling power rose by about 4% for the first 15 days, then the advantage faded. The biggest effect was on
    day 1, and it shrank steadily after that.
  - No competing interests were declared.
  - **Strength: limited (n = 11, cycling, not lifting).**

## 5. CYP1A2 and genotype claims

- **The largest meta-analysis.** Barreto2024 (ABSTRACT): 13 studies, 440 participants.
  - AA ("fast"): SMD 0.30 (0.21–0.39).
  - AC: SMD 0.16 (0.06–0.25).
  - CC: SMD −0.22 (−0.44 to −0.01), i.e. slightly worse.
- **The CC result is shaky.**
  - Only 34 people had the CC genotype.
  - Higher doses and a longer wait before exercise reduced the negative effect in CC.
  - Studies whose authors reported conflicts of interest affected the CC estimate.
  - The authors themselves urge caution.
- **A second meta-analysis disagrees.** Wang2024 (FULL; doi 10.1016/j.jshs.2023.12.005; not in CSV) found an effect
  only for A-allele cycling time trials and no genotype effect on Wingate or jump performance.
- **Strength: limited.** Most people (AA/AC) respond. Paying for a gene test to decide whether to use caffeine is not
  justified. A trial-and-error test in training tells you more.
- **Commercial tie.** The ISSN position stand's lead author consults for Nutrigenomix, a genetic-testing company
  (Guest2021 COI). Treat genotype-testing marketing with scepticism.

## 6. Multi-ingredient pre-workouts (MIPS): evidence and safety

- **What is in them.** Jagim2019 (ABSTRACT; doi 10.3390/nu11020254; not in CSV) analysed the top 100 products.
  - They average 18 ingredients.
  - 44% of ingredients sit in "proprietary blends" with undisclosed amounts.
  - Caffeine appears in 86% of products, averaging 254 ± 80 mg (about 2.9 mg/kg for Kye).
  - Beta-alanine is under-dosed (about 2 g on average).
  - In practice, a pre-workout is mostly a caffeine delivery system. **Strength: moderate (label audit).**
- **Acute effects.** Harty2018 (FULL review) found mainly better muscular endurance and mood, with mixed effects on
  force and power. Most trials test one product, often funded or supplied by its maker. **Strength: limited.**
- **Long-term training adaptations.**
  - Ormsbee2012 (FULL) found +2.8 kg lean mass vs +1.3 kg over 6 weeks in 24 trained men. But:
    - it was funded by and used product donated from the manufacturer (Vital Pharmaceuticals);
    - the supplement contained whey, casein, BCAAs and creatine;
    - the placebo was maltodextrin;
    - so any benefit is confounded by extra protein and creatine, not caffeine or a "pre-workout effect".
  - Nelson2019 (FULL): a MusclePharm product, with the placebo prepared by the manufacturer. Over 7 weeks there was no
    difference in fat-free mass or strength (n = 19 women).
  - **Strength: limited, and confounded.** Nothing shows that a stimulant pre-workout grows more muscle than its
    creatine and protein content would.
- **Safety and contamination: a safety issue only, no dosing or sourcing.**
  - Cohen2021 (ABSTRACT): 17 US sports and weight-loss supplement brands labelled with deterenol contained 9
    prohibited stimulants. These included 1,3-DMAA, octodrine (DMHA), BMPEA, oxilofrine and higenamine, with up to 4
    experimental stimulants in one product. They have never been tested in humans. The paper has a 2022 publisher
    correction (Crossref); its content was not checked.
  - Cohen2018 (SECONDARY, via news reports of the JAMA Intern Med research letter; doi 10.1001/jamainternmed.2018.4846;
    not in CSV) found that years after US FDA warnings, about 75% of re-purchased products still contained at least
    one prohibited stimulant. The authors were sued by a supplement company and won.
  - Case reports link pre-workouts to MI, demand ischaemia, rhabdomyolysis and pancreatitis. These are search hits,
    not appraised, and do not prove cause.
  - **Strength: moderate that contamination and illegal stimulants are a real, recurring problem.** Banned stimulants
    such as DMAA and DMHA are prohibited by WADA in competition, and DMAA is a prohibited substance in Australia
    (SECONDARY, general regulatory knowledge, not re-verified this run).

## 7. Does the acute boost turn into more muscle or strength over time?

- **Giráldez-Costas2021** (FULL): 4 weeks (12 sessions) of bench training, n = 16 (9 caffeine, 7 placebo).
  - 3 mg/kg before every session.
  - 1RM gain was the same: +13.5 ± 7.8% vs +11.3 ± 5.3% (p = 0.53).
  - Velocity improved across more loads in the caffeine group.
  - Tiny, short, and no hypertrophy measures.
- **Pakulak2022** (ABSTRACT): 6 weeks, 28 trained adults across 4 arms (only 6 in the caffeine-only arm).
  - 3 mg/kg/day caffeine.
  - Caffeine did not change muscle thickness, lean mass, strength or endurance vs placebo.
  - Only creatine improved knee-extensor thickness.
- **No longer or larger RCT in trained lifters was found** that measures hypertrophy with caffeine alone.
- **Strength: very limited.** The current evidence shows no added long-term strength or muscle gain. "No evidence of
  benefit" is not the same as "proven no benefit": the trials are far too small to detect a 1–2% difference.

---

## Verdict

- **It works, but the effect is small.** Caffeine is one of the few supplements that reliably improves a lifting
  session:
  - about +1–2% on 1RM (SMD about 0.2);
  - about one extra rep per set at moderate loads (SMD about 0.3);
  - faster bar speed.
- **Low doses are enough.** About 2–3 mg/kg captures most of the benefit. Going higher mostly buys side effects.
- **Habitual use blunts it partly, not fully.**
- **Genotype tests are not worth buying.**
- **Pre-workouts are mostly caffeine plus under-dosed extras and hidden blends.** They carry a documented
  contamination risk, including banned amphetamine-like stimulants.
- **There is no evidence that the session boost turns into more muscle or strength over months.**
- **The sleep cost is real and dose- and timing-dependent.** It is probably the biggest practical downside for someone
  training 1.5 h a day.

## Practical recommendation for Kye

1. **Dose.** If you use it, take about **175–260 mg (2–3 mg/kg)** about 45–60 min before your hardest sessions. That
   is one strong coffee or one caffeine tablet. Don't go above about 260 mg in a single dose, and keep your whole day
   at **≤400 mg**, coffee and energy drinks included.
2. **Save it for when it matters.** Use it on heavy or key sessions, PR attempts, or days you're flat. Daily use leads
   to partial tolerance within about 2–3 weeks (Lara2019). Skipping it for a week before a test day is reasonable but
   unproven for lifting.
3. **Protect your sleep. This matters more than the 1–2%.**
   - Aim to have the last caffeine **≥8–10 h before bed**. If you train in the evening, use none or ≤1 mg/kg
     (about 90 mg).
   - If you lift at 6 pm and sleep at 10:30 pm, a 300 mg scoop will very likely cost you sleep.
4. **Skip branded pre-workouts.** Plain caffeine plus creatine (separately) gives you all the evidence-based effect.
   If you use a pre-workout anyway, pick a fully disclosed label with no proprietary blend, tested by Informed Sport or
   HASTA. Avoid anything listing DMAA, DMHA/octodrine, "geranium extract", BMPEA, deterenol or similar.
5. **Don't expect it to grow muscle.** It helps you do slightly more on the day. Growth still comes from effort,
   volume, progression, protein and sleep.

## New avenues

1. Does chronic caffeine-induced sleep loss (about 30–45 min a night) measurably reduce hypertrophy or strength gains
   in trained lifters over 8 or more weeks? Any RCTs?
2. Does a 7–14-day caffeine withdrawal restore the full ergogenic effect in heavy habitual users for resistance
   exercise, not cycling?
3. Is there an RCT of 8 weeks or more in trained lifters comparing caffeine with placebo on whole-session volume
   load and muscle thickness, with blinding checked?
4. What do Australian (TGA/FSANZ) and recent Informed Sport/HASTA testing data show about stimulant contamination
   rates in pre-workouts sold in Australia (2022–2026)?

**Framing note on dose (no number changed).** Recommendation 1's single-dose ceiling of "about 260 mg" is 3 mg/kg for
87 kg. That is consistent with EFSA's per-kg basis, but **above EFSA's absolute 200 mg single-dose figure**. Kye should
know that 175–200 mg sits inside both, and that 260 mg is a weight-scaled extrapolation. The daily ceiling of ≤400 mg
matches EFSA.

**Safety.** No dosing or sourcing is given for any banned stimulant. DMAA, DMHA and the others are named only as
things to avoid. The recommendations are safe.

**Verdict stands.** Nothing load-bearing was wrong.
