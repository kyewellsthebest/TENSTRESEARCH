## Plain-English summary (10 lines)

1. **Fasted cardio burns more fat during the session, and it does not make you lose more fat.** A meta-analysis
   puts the extra at about 3 g per session. Over 4–6 weeks, trials find no difference in fat lost (5 trials, 96 people).
2. **The body does not "switch" to fat once glucose is gone.** It always burns a mix of fuels, and the mix
   shifts toward whatever you have eaten. What sets fat loss over days and weeks is the energy deficit.
3. **The best direct test goes against the idea.** In a metabolic ward, cutting carbs raised fat burning and
   lowered insulin. With calories equal, it still removed *less* body fat than cutting fat did (53 vs 89 g/day).
4. **No good trial shows that carb cycling at the same weekly calories loses more fat.** The one direct test
   found is an unpublished 12-person student thesis, and it showed no difference.
5. **Feeling "flat" on low-carb days is mostly lost glycogen and the water stored with it, not fat.** One trial
   recorded a 4.8% jump in measured "lean mass" in a single week of eating carbs again.
6. **You need less carbohydrate to lift well than gym lore says.** In a fed state, at up to about 10 hard sets
   per muscle, carb intake makes little measurable difference to strength. Carbs help most in high-volume
   sessions and against training fasted.
7. **Diet breaks and refeeds in trained lifters did not add fat loss.** They did reduce hunger and may help
   people stick to the diet. The big "diet breaks work" result (MATADOR) came from obese untrained men.
8. **16:8 fasting and keto do not hurt short-term strength, and they do not burn fat by magic.** Where they do
   better, the likely reason is that people ate less. On keto, apparent lean-mass losses are partly water.
9. **Protein matters more than carb timing.** In a deficit, aim for about 1.6 g/kg at minimum, and 2.0–2.4 g/kg
   is reasonable for a lean lifter (Helms: 2.3–3.1 g/kg of *fat-free* mass).
10. **Verdict.** Eating fewer carbs on rest days to be "flat" does not cause more fat loss than spreading the
    same weekly calories evenly. Carb cycling is fine if it helps you stick to the deficit or puts carbs around
    hard sessions. The mechanism behind it is wrong.

## How the evidence is graded

The project's evidence codes were written for EMG work, so I have adapted them for this topic:

- **D**: a long-term body-composition RCT in resistance-trained people.
- **I**: a long-term trial in another population (untrained, obese, women only, endurance athletes).
- **M**: acute substrate oxidation, chamber, or mechanistic work.

Strength is graded strong / moderate / limited / very limited / none. Keys refer to `W2-studies.csv`.

## 1. Fasted vs fed cardio

**What happens in the session: Kye is right about this part.** In the fasted state, fat oxidation during
aerobic exercise is higher, by about 3 g per session. The 95% CI is roughly 0.8–5.4 g (Vieira2016, M, moderate).
3 g of fat is about 27 kcal. Fasted exercise also raises free fatty acids after exercise (Aird2018, M).

**What happens over 24 hours: interesting, and not decisive.** Iwayama's group measured people in a whole-room
metabolic chamber.

- In 10 men, 60 min at 50% VO2max *before breakfast* raised 24-h fat oxidation to 717 kcal/day, against
  456 kcal/day sedentary. The same exercise after lunch (446) or after dinner (432) did not raise it
  (Iwayama2015).
- Women showed the same pattern (Iwayama2017).
- The size of the effect tracked the transient carbohydrate deficit the session created (Iwayama2020).

The caveats are large:

- These were **energy-balanced** days, with n of about 10.
- 24-h fat oxidation is not fat-mass change. The body can store more fat on the following days.
- When intake is raised to cover the exercise, 24-h fat oxidation returns to its sedentary level. This is the
  Melanson 2009 chamber work, seen only as a citation inside the Iwayama papers (Melanson2009, SECONDARY).

The opposite acute result also exists. In 8 trained men, EPOC was higher at 12 and 24 h after *fed* exercise,
and the authors concluded that fasting does not enhance lipid use (Paoli2011, M, very limited).

**What happens to fat mass over weeks: Kye is wrong about this part.**

| trial | who | what | result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schoenfeld2014 | 20 women | 4 wk in a deficit, cardio 3×/wk, fasted vs fed | same fat loss |
| Gillen2013 | 16 overweight women | 6 wk of HIIT, fasted vs fed | same improvements |
| Hackett2017 | meta-analysis, 5 studies, 96 people | fasted vs fed training | trivial between-group effect on body mass, %fat and lean mass |

The authors of Hackett2017 say there are too few data to be sure.

The evidence has gaps. **No trial is in resistance-trained men. All are short and all are underpowered.** A
null result here is not proof of equivalence. What the data do show is that any long-term advantage is too small
for these trials to detect. Grade: **I, limited**, and consistent across trials.

**The one place fasted training does show a difference is metabolic health, not fat.** In men with overweight,
6 weeks of fasted-state cycling improved oral glucose insulin sensitivity. Fasted training gave
+25 ± 38 mL/min/m², against −21 ± 32 for fed training (P = 0.01), along with more intramuscular lipid use
(Edinburgh2020, I, limited). Acute adipose-tissue gene expression is also higher fasted (Chen2017, M). Neither
finding is fat-loss evidence.

**The cost for a lifter.** Feeding before exercise improves long aerobic performance (Aird2018). It is the fasted
control condition that produces most of the "carbs help lifting" results (Henselmans2022). Lifting or doing hard
intervals fasted is therefore the setting where performance is most likely to suffer.

## 2. Why "glucose first, then fat" is the wrong model (mechanism)

- **Fuel use is a continuous mix, not a switch.** Carbohydrate oxidation rises and falls with carbohydrate
  intake, because the body keeps glycogen stores tightly regulated. Glycogen capacity is roughly 15 g/kg, and
  about 500 g of carbohydrate goes into storage before net de novo lipogenesis begins in massive overfeeding
  (Acheson1988, M; n = 3).
- **Fat balance = fat oxidised − fat eaten.** Over days, total energy balance decides it. If you eat fewer
  carbs, you oxidise more fat, but you are usually eating more fat too. If you eat more carbs, you oxidise more
  carbs and store more of the dietary fat you eat.
- **The cleanest test is Hall2015** (metabolic ward, n = 19, crossover). Cutting 30% of calories from carbs
  raised fat oxidation and lowered insulin, and produced **53 ± 6 g/day** of body-fat loss. Cutting the same
  calories from fat left fat oxidation unchanged, yet produced **89 ± 6 g/day** of loss. In other words, burning
  more fat in the moment and losing more body fat are separate things. (M/I, moderate. Caveats: 6 days, people
  with obesity.)
- **Across 32 controlled-feeding studies**, equal-calorie lower-fat diets gave slightly *more* energy
  expenditure (+26 kcal/day) and fat loss (+16 g/day) than lower-carb diets (HallGuo2017). The effect is
  practically zero in either direction. Ludwig's 2021 reanalysis disputes the direction; I have not opened it.
- **An isocaloric ketogenic diet** raised chamber energy expenditure only transiently, by about 60–100 kcal/day
  (Hall2016). I have not confirmed the body-fat result from this search; see the CSV.

**Verdict on Kye's point 2: partly right acutely, wrong where it matters.** Glycogen does influence fuel choice,
and fasted exercise does burn more fat in the session. Fat mass is set by the energy deficit over days, not by
which fuel was burned in one hour. Grade: **moderate.**

## 3. Carb cycling and carbohydrate periodisation

- **Direct RCTs of carb cycling in lifters, with weekly calories matched.** I found essentially none in the
  peer-reviewed literature. The only near-direct test is a Brazilian undergraduate thesis (UFPE_TCC_carbcycling):
  12 male bodybuilders, 6 weeks in a deficit, carb cycling vs control. It found no difference in weight, fat or
  lean mass. It is not peer reviewed, and I have read only its abstract (D, very limited).
- **Calorie cycling** in overweight untrained women: Davoodi2014 (n = 74, 6 weeks). Both arms lost weight.
  Cycling reported better adherence and satiety, and RMR tended to hold. This is calorie cycling, not carb
  cycling, and the head-to-head weight result was not confirmed. The Kreider-lab "Curves" cycling work was funded
  through a commercial programme (Kreider_Curves, SECONDARY, COI).
- **Train-low / periodisation research is about endurance adaptation, not fat loss.** "Fuel for the work
  required" (Impey2018) is a framework for glycogen-dependent signalling in endurance athletes. In its tally,
  train-low augmented signalling in 73% of 11 studies, but performance improved in only 37% of 11.
- **Sleep low** (Marquet2016): 21 triathletes kept the same daily carbohydrate and only changed its timing. The
  sleep-low group improved 10-km time and lost more fat: −8.5% ± 7.4 against −2.6% ± 7.4. This is the strongest
  "timing changes fat" result found. Its limits:
  - n = 21 and 3 weeks.
  - Fat mass was a secondary outcome.
  - Intake was free-living.
  - The subjects were endurance athletes doing double sessions, with fasted low-intensity sessions on top.
  - Replications were not searched here.

  I would not transfer it to a lifter's rest days (I, very limited).
- **What "flat" is.** Low-carb days lower muscle glycogen, and glycogen is stored with water. Body weight and
  muscle fullness drop quickly and come back quickly. That is not fat. Two results in this search show the size
  of it:
  - In Wilson2020, one week of carb reintroduction after keto raised DXA "lean body mass" by **4.8%**.
  - In Peos2021_PLOS, a 1-week diet break raised body weight and FFM with no change in fat.

  The ~3 g water per g glycogen figure is widely quoted but was **not verified** in this search.
- **Refeeds and diet breaks in trained people:**
  - Campbell2020 (n = 27 resistance-trained, 7 weeks, 2-day high-carb refeed each week): no fat-loss advantage.
    Only dry FFM differed on reanalysis. The authors concede that FFM and RMR were not significantly different.
  - ICECAP (Peos2021_ICECAP, n = 61 resistance-trained athletes, 12 weeks of restriction; IER was 3 weeks of
    restriction then 1 week at maintenance): similar fat loss and FFM, with less appetite on IER.
  - JHK2023_dietbreaks (trained women): no body-composition or RMR advantage.

  Grade: **D, moderate**. Diet breaks do not add fat loss in trained people. They may help hunger and
  adherence.
- **MATADOR** (Byrne2018): 51 obese men randomised, 2-week blocks alternating restriction and maintenance. INT
  lost more fat, 12.3 vs 8.0 kg. Caveats: untrained obese men, a completer analysis, INT took about twice as long
  in calendar time, and the result has not been replicated in trained people (I, limited).

**Verdict on Kye's point 3, the carb-cycling part: wrong as a fat-loss mechanism, harmless as a tactic.** No
controlled evidence shows that being "flat" on rest days produces extra fat loss at matched weekly calories. The
mechanism argument (section 2) predicts it should not. Low-carb days raise fat oxidation on those days. High-carb
days lower it. Over the week, fat balance tracks the weekly energy deficit. Grade: **limited** (no direct
trials); the mechanism evidence is **moderate**.

## 4. Do lifters need carbs to fuel sessions?

- **Henselmans2022** (systematic review of 49 studies):
  - Acute effect: none in 13 studies, a benefit in 6. The benefits came mostly against fasted controls and in
    sessions over 10 sets per muscle.
  - Long term: no effect in 15 of 17 studies.
  - No dose-response.
  - The authors note that every positive study also had higher energy intake.
- **Escobar2016** (narrative review): the 3–7 g/kg/day recommendation is borrowed from endurance research, and
  the evidence for it in resistance training is thin.
- **Keto trials keep strength in the short term:**
  - Greene2018: 14 powerlifters and weightlifters, 3 months, lifts unchanged.
  - Vargas-Molina2020: 21 trained women, 8 weeks. Fat lost, FFM did not rise.
  - Kephart2018: CrossFit pilot, n = 12. 1RM squat similar, LDL +35%.
  - Wilson2020: 25 trained men.
- **The ketogenic-diet meta-analysis** reports more fat loss and also more FFM loss, about −1.26 kg (secondary
  summary), with keto (AshtaryLarky2022). Part of the FFM loss is water and glycogen, and keto arms often ate
  less.

**Verdict: partly right.** Carbs around training help in high-volume sessions, in long sessions, and when you
would otherwise train fasted. On a normal hypertrophy session done fed, how many carbs you eat matters much less
than total calories and protein. Putting carbs before and after training is sensible. It is a performance and
adherence choice, not a fat-loss mechanism. Grade: **moderate** for "carbs per se matter little for ≤10 sets
done fed"; **limited** for high-volume sessions.

## 5. Intermittent fasting / time-restricted eating in lifters

- **Moro2016**: 34 trained men, 8 weeks of 16/8. Fat mass was lower than on a normal diet (p = 0.045), with
  strength, FFM and arm/thigh muscle area maintained. T3 fell.
- **Tinsley2019**: 40 trained women. FFM gain and hypertrophy were similar across arms. Fat changes were
  significant per-protocol only, not intention-to-treat.
- **Lowe2020 (TREAT)**: 116 adults, no resistance training. No difference in weight between arms.
- **Meta-analyses**: Ho2024 (7 RCTs, n = 164) found TRF + RT gave −1.52 kg fat mass (95% CI −2.30 to −0.75),
  with no change in muscle or strength. A 2025 meta-analysis (15 studies) found a small fat-mass reduction with
  FFM preserved.

Intake was not matched in most of these trials, so the fat effect is best explained by eating less (I/D,
limited). TRE is a valid adherence tool for a lifter and does not appear to cost muscle or strength over 8 weeks.

## 6. Protein in a deficit (more important than any of the above)

- **Morton2018** (49 RCTs, mostly in energy balance): FFM gains plateau at about **1.62 g/kg/day**. The 95% CI is
  wide, 1.03–2.20, so treat 1.6 as a floor, not a ceiling.
- **Helms2014** (6 studies, lean trained people in a deficit): recommends **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass**,
  rising as the deficit deepens and the athlete gets leaner. The evidence base is small.
- **Longland2016** (n = 40, a 40% deficit with all food provided, RT + HIIT 6 days/week): 2.4 g/kg gave
  +1.2 kg LBM and −4.8 kg FM, against +0.1 kg and −3.5 kg at 1.2 g/kg. This was a huge deficit in non-lifters.

Grade: **moderate** that protein of at least 1.6 g/kg, and plausibly up to about 2.4 g/kg, protects lean mass in
a deficit.

## 7. Kye's model, point by point

| claim | verdict | why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Fat loss = 200–300 kcal/day deficit | **Right in principle** (energy balance is what drives fat loss); the size of the deficit is outside W2's scope | Sections 2 and 3: every macro or timing manipulation collapses to energy balance when calories are matched |
| 2. Carbs burned first, fat only once glycogen is "flattened"; hence fasted cardio | **Partly right acutely, wrong for fat loss** | Fasted sessions burn about 3 g more fat; 24-h fat oxidation is higher only on energy-balanced chamber days; 4–6 week trials show no extra fat loss; cutting carbs raised fat oxidation yet lost less fat (Hall2015) |
| 3a. Lifters need carbs to fuel sessions | **Partly right** | They matter for high volume and over fasting; little effect for ≤10 sets done fed |
| 3b. Low carbs on rest days to be "flat" burns more fat | **Wrong as a mechanism; no supporting trial** | "Flat" is glycogen and water. Weekly fat balance follows the weekly deficit. Refeed and diet-break RCTs in trained people show no added fat loss |

**What to do instead**:

- Set the weekly deficit.
- Keep protein at 1.6–2.4 g/kg.
- Put most carbs around the hardest sessions if you like. Fewer carbs on rest days is fine as long as the weekly
  calories stay the same.
- Do cardio at whatever time you will actually do it.
- Judge progress by weekly average weight and waist, not by how "flat" you look. Scale weight on low-carb days
  will mislead you by a kilogram or more.

## 8. Gaps and evidence-quality flags

- No fasted-vs-fed or carb-cycling body-composition RCT in resistance-trained men longer than 8 weeks was found.
- Small samples throughout: Iwayama n ≈ 10, Paoli n = 8, Gillen 8 per group, Kephart 12, Greene 14.
- Ad libitum designs confound the keto and TRE results, because the "diet" arm usually ate less.
- DXA and BIA lean mass is confounded by glycogen and water. This applies to every keto and refeed result.
- Disputes:
  - Campbell2020 is subject to a published reanalysis letter.
  - Greene2018 drew a published methodological comment.
  - Hall2016 is disputed by Friedman & Appel 2019.
  - HallGuo2017 is disputed by Ludwig 2021.
- COI: the Kreider "Curves" work is commercially linked. Possible industry ties in Wilson2020 were not verified.
- Not verified this session and kept out of the conclusions: Tinsley2017, Melanson2009 (citation only), Hall2016's
  body-fat outcome, and the testosterone result in Moro2016.
