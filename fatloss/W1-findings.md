# W1 — Energy balance, fuel use and the "glucose first, then fat" model

Agent W1, 2026-11-04. Tests Kye's claims 1 and 2 from `BRIEF.md`.

## Ten-line summary (plain English)

1. **Claim 1 is mostly right.** Fat loss is driven by an energy deficit held over weeks. Ward studies (participants live on a research unit and all food is weighed) back this. When calories are matched, swapping carbs for fat moves body fat by grams per day, not kilograms.
2. **But 200–300 kcal/day is small for a trained man, and it may be too small to measure.** At best it is about 0.2–0.3 kg of fat a week (about 0.25–0.35 % of body weight a week for an assumed 85 kg man). That is below the 0.5–1 %/week usually recommended for lifters. The bigger problem is error: calorie estimates and the body's own adaptation can each swallow 200–300 kcal.
3. **The slow rate does protect muscle.** Losing 0.7 %/week kept lean mass better than 1.4 %/week in athletes. A meta-analysis also found lean-mass gains suffer once the deficit goes past roughly 500 kcal/day.
4. **Claim 2 is wrong as a mechanism.** The body does not burn glucose first and switch to fat only once glycogen is "flattened". Fat is burned all the time. At rest after an overnight fast it supplies most of the energy, and during easy-to-moderate exercise it gives the most it ever does, with glycogen still full.
5. **What sets the fuel mix is mainly exercise intensity, then training status, diet and timing.** Harder work burns a larger share of carbohydrate (the crossover concept). A hard lifting session empties only about a quarter to two-fifths of the glycogen in the working muscle. Nobody lifts their way to empty.
6. **The part that is right:** carbs in the bloodstream and the insulin they raise do shift the mix toward carbohydrate. Low-carb eating and fasted exercise genuinely raise fat burning, measured by gas exchange.
7. **But burning more fat is not the same as losing more fat.** On a ward, cutting carbs raised fat burning yet lost less body fat than cutting the same calories from fat (53 vs 89 g/day). A ketogenic diet raised fat burning, and body-fat loss slowed.
8. **Fasted cardio shows the same thing.** It raises fat burning during the session and sometimes across 24 h. Trials measuring body composition over weeks find no extra fat loss, because what is burned in the session is balanced out over the rest of the day.
9. **The scale misleads.** Each gram of glycogen is stored with at least 3 g of water. Cutting carbs or going "flat" can drop 1–2 kg in days that is not fat, and refeeding puts it back. On a 0.25 kg/week fat-loss target, that noise is several weeks of real progress.
10. **Bottom line for Kye:** keep the deficit and drop the fuel-switch story. Size the deficit by the weekly trend of a 7-day average of morning weight (aim 0.5–1 %/week, or the low end if muscle matters most). Put carbs where they help training. Do not expect being "flat" to burn extra fat.

## 2. Fuel use: fat is burned continuously

**Indirect calorimetry and RER/RQ.** These measure the CO2 breathed out against the O2 taken in. The ratio is about 0.70 when only fat is burned and 1.00 when only carbohydrate is burned. It is how every "fat oxidation" number below was obtained. Two caveats:

- It reads whole-body **net** oxidation, so it says nothing about where the fat came from.
- During hard exercise, CO2 from bicarbonate buffering inflates it.

A review of 25 studies (Springer/IJO, ijo.2015.29; author not seen, SECONDARY) found fasting RQ rises from about 0.80 toward 0.90 as dietary carbohydrate goes up. A post-absorptive RQ of about 0.8 means fat already supplies the majority of resting energy, with glycogen stores intact. That alone contradicts "glucose first, fat only after".

**Exercise intensity.** These findings show fat use is highest at moderate intensity with full glycogen:

- **Romijn 1993** (n = 5 trained, stable-isotope tracers): fat released into the blood is greatest at 25 % VO2max. Plasma fatty acids supply most of the fuel at 25 % and 65 %. Muscle glycogen dominates at 85 %. Fat oxidation is highest at moderate intensity, with full glycogen, in the first hour. Over 2 h at 65 %, the share from plasma fuels rose as muscle glycogen use fell. Glycogen depletion **does** push the mix further toward fat late in long sessions, but fat was already the main fuel before that.
- **Brooks & Mercier 1994**, crossover concept: the share of carbohydrate rises with intensity, and endurance training shifts the crossover to higher intensities. The mix depends on intensity × training status, not on whether glycogen is "used up". A later chamber series puts the average crossover at about 55 % VO2max, with wide spread between individuals (SECONDARY). Coggan published a 1997 critique (title seen only).
- **Achten, Gleeson & Jeukendrup 2002** (n = 18 trained cyclists): FATmax at 64 ± 4 % VO2max (74 ± 3 % HRmax). The zone within 10 % of peak runs from 55 to 72 % VO2max. A 2003 reliability study reported MFO of 0.52 ± 0.15 g/min at 62.5 % VO2max (SECONDARY). A peak of about 0.5 g/min is about 4.5 kcal/min. Even an hour at FATmax burns roughly 30 g of fat, and it is the 24-h balance that decides whether any of it is net loss.

**Resistance training.** A lifting session does not empty glycogen:

- A 2025 meta-analysis (Physiol Rep, doi 10.14814/phy2.70683; 20 studies, 180 participants, vastus lateralis) found more sets and longer sessions deplete more glycogen, and higher intensity per set depletes **less**. Trained lifters deplete less than untrained.
- Earlier work commonly cited 24–40 % depletion (MacDougall 1988; Robergs 1991; Tesch). A biopsy study found depletion by fibre type of 23 / 40 / 44 % in type I / IIa / IIx fibres.

A lifter never "flattens" glycogen by training. Fat is still oxidised between sets, after the session and overnight.

## 3. Why acute fat oxidation ≠ fat loss (24-h compensation)

**Fat balance, not fat burning, is what changes fat mass.** Carbohydrate oxidation tracks carbohydrate intake within about 24 h, but fat oxidation does not rise to match a fat surplus (Schutz, Flatt & Jéquier 1989: a ~106 g fat supplement did not raise 24-h fat oxidation; Flatt 1985). So:

- The body keeps carbohydrate balanced.
- Whatever energy is left over, positive or negative, ends up in the fat store.
- **Over time, fat balance ≈ energy balance.**

This is the mechanism behind "the deficit drives loss". It is also why the fuel mix in one session cannot by itself set fat loss.

**Chamber studies** (24 h in a sealed calorimeter room):

- **Melanson 2009** (J Appl Physiol 107:1847): 1 h at 55 % VO2max, with the energy spent on exercise replaced by food. 24-h fat oxidation did not differ from a rest day in lean sedentary, obese sedentary or endurance-trained adults, and fat balance was if anything *more positive* on the exercise day. The fat burned during exercise was offset by less fat burned for the rest of the day.
- **Melanson 2002** (SECONDARY): low- vs high-intensity exercise days gave no difference in 24-h fat oxidation.
- **Hansen, Shriver & Schoeller 2005** (Sports Med review): 24-h fat balance is what matters. Moderate intensity gives the largest cumulative fat use.

**The one real exception: timing.** In **Iwayama 2015** (n = 10 men, energy balanced), exercise *before breakfast* raised 24-h fat oxidation to 717 ± 64 kcal/day, against 456 ± 61 at control. Afternoon and evening exercise did not raise it. A 2017 study in women replicated this (about 400 → 519 kcal/day, SECONDARY), and so did Shimada 2013 in athletes (SECONDARY). These are small, short studies (one day each) in energy balance. Higher fat oxidation at energy balance has to be paired with storage elsewhere, carbohydrate surplus becoming glycogen, so they do not show fat-mass loss. **Hackett & Hagstrom 2017** pooled body-composition outcomes (5 studies, n = 96): the fasted vs fed difference in body mass was trivial. **Schoenfeld 2014** (n = 20 women, 4 weeks, hypocaloric) found no between-group difference. **Vieira 2016** (BJN meta-analysis, SECONDARY only) found fasted aerobic exercise raises fat oxidation during the session. Fasted cardio does what Kye says acutely, and the fat-loss payoff has not been shown. The trials are small and in mixed or untrained, mostly female, samples, so "no difference" is not proof of equivalence. A small effect cannot be excluded.

## 4. Ward diet trials: isocaloric low-carb vs low-fat

These test claim 2 head-on. If carbs blocked fat loss, cutting carbs at equal calories should lose more fat.

- **Hall 2015, Cell Metab** (doi 10.1016/j.cmet.2015.07.021), n = 19 adults with obesity, ward, crossover.
  - Design: 5-day baseline (2,720 kcal, 50 % carbohydrate), then 6 days at –30 % energy by cutting either carbs or fat (about 800 kcal/day).
  - The carbohydrate cut lowered insulin and raised 24-h fat oxidation. The fat cut changed neither.
  - **Fat loss by fat balance: 53 ± 6 g/day (carb cut) vs 89 ± 6 g/day (fat cut).**
  - Caveats: DXA did not resolve the difference, the study was short and the sample had obesity. The model predicts the gap narrows over the long term.
  - This is the clearest demonstration that more fat burned ≠ more fat lost. (The size of the fat-oxidation rise, recalled as roughly 460 kcal/day, is UNVERIFIED; not seen in any retrieved text.)
- **Hall 2016, AJCN** (NCT01967563), n = 17 overweight or obese men on a ward.
  - Design: 4 weeks on a high-carb baseline diet, then 4 weeks isocaloric ketogenic (15:5:80 protein:carb:fat, protein held fixed).
  - Chamber energy expenditure rose about 57 kcal/day, briefly. Doubly labelled water read higher, reanalysed as 151 kcal/day (Friedman & Appel preprint).
  - **Body-fat loss slowed on the ketogenic diet,** coinciding with more protein use and fat-free mass loss.
  - The diet order was not randomised. Hall's 2019 PLoS ONE reanalysis found non-chamber energy expenditure not significantly different (3,395 ± 766 vs 3,209 ± 642 kcal/day).
- **Hall & Guo 2017, Gastroenterology:** meta-analysis of controlled-feeding isocaloric studies. Lower-fat diets gave slightly **higher** energy expenditure (+26 kcal/day) and **more** fat loss (+16 g/day). That is the opposite sign to the carbohydrate-insulin model and trivially small either way. The study count differs by source (20 or 32 studies); UNVERIFIED which subset applies.
- **Hall 2021, Nat Med:** crossover, n = 20, inpatient, ad libitum, 2 weeks per diet. The plant-based low-fat diet was eaten at **689 ± 73 kcal/day less** than the animal-based ketogenic diet, and only the low-fat diet significantly reduced body fat. This shows that freely chosen intake, not fuel use, decides outcomes outside the ward. It was not a weight-loss trial.
- **DIETFITS, Gardner 2018, JAMA** (n = 609, 12 months, free-living):
  - Weight change was –5.3 kg on healthy low-fat vs –6.0 kg on healthy low-carb. Difference 0.7 kg (95 % CI –0.2 to 1.6).
  - Neither insulin secretion (P = .47) nor genotype predicted which diet worked.
  - Individuals ranged from about –30 to +10 kg in both arms. Adherence to a deficit matters far more than the macro split.
- **Ebbeling 2018, BMJ** (Ludwig group, doubly labelled water, 20-week maintenance after weight loss):
  - Low-carb (20 % carbohydrate) vs high-carb (60 %): TEE +209 kcal/day (ITT; 95 % CI 91 to 326) and +278 per protocol.
  - **Contested.** The pre-registered analysis (baseline before weight loss) showed no significant diet effect (Hall & Speakman 2019, Nutr Diabetes). The effect depended on the post-weight-loss baseline adopted after data collection, and on participants with large unexplained energy discrepancies.
  - The Ludwig group replied that the change was made before unblinding and that a confounding-adjusted reanalysis upholds the effect.
  - Treat it as **unresolved, at most a modest effect, and in maintenance, not in a deficit**. It does not support "fat burns only once glycogen is flat".
- **Debate framing:**
  - Ludwig 2021 (AJCN 114:1873): the carbohydrate–insulin model says high-glycaemic-load diets drive fat storage, which then drives overeating.
  - Hall 2022 (AJCN 115:1243): the energy balance model says energy balance is a physical law that is agnostic about mechanism, and the brain-centred EBM explains the data better.
  - Even the CIM accepts that fat is lost only through an energy deficit. The dispute is about **why people overeat**, not whether a deficit is required.
  - For a lean lifter weighing his food, the debate is largely moot.

## 5. Deficit size and rate of loss for lean trained men

**Rate studies:**

- **Garthe 2011, IJSNEM 21:97** (n = 24 elite athletes, 4 resistance-training sessions/week):
  - 0.7 %/week vs 1.4 %/week loss.
  - Slow arm: lean mass +2.1 %, fast arm about 0 (p < .01).
  - Fat loss 31 % vs 21–23 % (sources differ).
  - Upper-body 1RM about +12 % vs +6 %.
  - The 12-month follow-up found no difference between groups. Small, mixed sports, partly female.
- **Helms 2014, JISSN 11:20** (narrative review): 0.5–1 %/week to keep muscle; protein 2.3–3.1 g/kg lean body mass; fat 15–30 % of energy; carbohydrate the remainder. The authors stress the data are "extremely limited".
- **Murphy & Koehler 2022, Scand J Med Sci Sports** (doi 10.1111/sms.14075):
  - Lifting in a deficit impaired lean-mass gains (ES –0.57, p = .02). Strength was not significantly impaired (ES –0.31, p = .28).
  - Meta-regression: about 500 kcal/day "prevented" lean-mass gain.
  - Mostly untrained samples. The threshold has been criticised as over-read (SECONDARY).
- **Roth, Schoenfeld & Behringer 2022, Eur J Appl Physiol** (review, 15 studies): higher weekly sets (≥10 per muscle) were associated with low-to-no lean-mass loss in a deficit, mainly in women. They judged the evidence insufficient. *(This is probably the "Roth 2023" in the request. No 2023 review was found.)*
- **Roth 2023, Scand J Med Sci Sports 33(1)**: RCT, n = 38 resistance-trained men in a deficit, 5 vs 3 sets per exercise. Lean mass **fell in both groups**, with no difference between them. Even trained men lose some lean mass in a deficit; volume did not prevent it.

**Arithmetic for Kye** (weight not stated, so 85 kg is assumed):

- 200–300 kcal/day is 1,400–2,100 kcal/week.
- Pure adipose tissue holds about 7,700 kcal/kg (fat ≈ 9 kcal/g; adipose ≈ 85 % lipid; standard figure, not separately searched). That gives an upper bound of about **0.18–0.27 kg/week**, or **0.2–0.3 %/week**: below Helms' band and about a third to half of Garthe's slow arm.
- The static "3,500 kcal per lb" rule overstates loss because spending falls as weight falls. Hall 2011 (Lancet) has the dynamic model, and the 3,500 rule overpredicted loss in 7 confinement studies (Thomas 2013, SECONDARY).

**Why 300 kcal is fragile:**

- **Measurement:** food-label and logging error, and smartwatch or formula estimates of TDEE, are each easily ±200–300 kcal/day (general knowledge; not quantified in this search pass, so treat as an estimate).
- **Adaptation:** in CALERIE, TDEE adjusted for body composition was lower by 431 ± 51 kcal/day at 3 months and 240 ± 83 at 6 months. Physical activity also fell (Redman 2009, n about 48 (UNVERIFIED), PLoS ONE, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0004377). Muscles were not trained and participants were not lean, but the direction applies.
- **Conclusion:** a nominal 250-kcal deficit can be zero in practice. The deficit has to be **defined by outcome** (trend weight falling about 0.5 %/week, with strength holding), not by a calculator.

**Verdict:** a small deficit is reasonable *if muscle retention is the priority and patience is high*. Most evidence-based guidance for trained men points to about 0.5–0.7 %/week. In practice that is usually more like a 400–600 kcal/day deficit, depending on size and activity. It should be confirmed against weight trend, not assumed.

## 6. Adaptive thermogenesis and NEAT

Adaptive thermogenesis is a fall in energy spending larger than the loss of body tissue explains.

- **Rosenbaum & Leibel 2010** (Int J Obes 34 S1:S47, review): keeping weight 10 % below usual comes with 24-h energy expenditure lower than body composition predicts. A review table gives about 15 % (UNVERIFIED in the text seen). Most of the gap is non-resting: lower muscle work efficiency and lower activity, partly reversed by leptin. It is seen in lean and obese people. A 2008 study by the group found the shortfall persists well after the active loss phase (SECONDARY).
- **Müller, Enderle & Bosy-Westphal 2016** (Curr Obes Rep, doi 10.1007/s13679-016-0237-4):
  - Most of the drop in energy expenditure follows lost fat-free mass.
  - Adaptive thermogenesis acts at two "set points": **early weight loss, linked to glycogen depletion**, and maintained reduced weight, linked to low leptin.
  - **Müller 2015 AJCN 102:807:** n = 32 lean young men; 1 week overfeeding at +50 %, then 3 weeks restriction at –50 %, then 2 weeks refeeding. Body weight changed +1.8, –6.0 and +3.5 kg. Skeletal muscle fell 5 % and liver 13 % during restriction. The mass-independent drop in resting energy expenditure reversed within 2 weeks of refeeding.
  - This is the one place where "glycogen" and "fat loss" really connect: **low glycogen and low carbohydrate intake send an early signal that slows metabolism**. If anything, that argues *against* chronically staying "flat".
- **Fothergill/Hall 2016** (Obesity, n = 14 Biggest Loser contestants): 6 years on, metabolic adaptation was –499 ± 207 kcal/day and RMR was 704 ± 427 kcal/day below baseline, after regaining 41 ± 31 kg. This is an extreme, public, rapid-loss setting and does not transfer to a 250-kcal cut. It shows that adaptation scales with the effort to stay below one's natural weight.
- **NEAT** (energy spent on everyday movement outside exercise): Levine 1999 found the rise in NEAT during overfeeding predicted who stayed lean (SECONDARY). On the restriction side, CALERIE found reduced physical activity (Redman 2009). For a lean lifter, unconscious drops in steps and fidgeting are the most likely way a 250-kcal deficit disappears. **Tracking daily steps is worth more than tracking session fat oxidation.**

## 7. Why the scale misleads, and what "flat" means

- **Glycogen holds water:**
  - Fernández-Elías 2015 (Eur J Appl Physiol 115:1919; n = 9 trained): each gram of muscle glycogen is stored with **at least 3 g of water**, and up to 1:17 when fluid is abundant (the extra water is mostly not bound to glycogen).
  - A 1982 starvation study found about 3.2 g of water per g on repletion (SECONDARY).
  - A 2018 sports-nutrition review attributes early low-carb weight loss mostly to glycogen-bound water (PMC6019055, SECONDARY).
  - Kreitzman 1992 ("glycogen storage: illusions of easy weight loss") is the classic source, but it was **not located**: UNVERIFIED.
- **Size of the effect:**
  - Whole-body glycogen in a fed trained man is roughly 400–600 g, mostly muscle plus about 100 g in the liver (textbook range; not separately searched).
  - With 3–4 g of water per gram, emptying or refilling it moves body weight by **about 1.5–3 kg**.
  - High-carb vs low-carb days in carb cycling will make the scale swing by around a kilogram day to day. That swing is 3–5 weeks of real fat loss at Kye's planned rate.
  - Ketogenic trials in lifters see "lean mass" fall on DXA without lost muscle protein: Vargas 2018 JISSN, n = 24 trained men. In a surplus, the ketogenic arm lost 0.8 kg fat and did not gain lean mass, while the non-ketogenic arm did. DXA counts glycogen water as lean mass.
- **What "flat" is:**
  - "Flat" is the look and feel of muscle when glycogen is low: less glycogen and water inside the muscle cells and a smaller muscle cross-section. It often comes with weaker pumps and lower capacity for many sets.
  - It is *not* a fat-burning state. It means low carbohydrate stores, and per section 3, fat mass still follows the weekly energy balance.
  - The reverse is measurable. In de Moraes 2019 (J Sports Sci Med 18:772; n = 24 bodybuilders, not randomised), carb-loading increased ultrasound muscle thickness and photo scores, at the cost of GI symptoms.
  - Being flat is mainly a water and glycogen effect.
- **Practical reading:** judge fat loss on 7-day averages of morning weight, compared week to week across matching phases of the carb cycle, plus waist and skinfold or photo trends. Do not read single days.

## 8. Implications for the Muscle Band

Outside this brief's scope; noted only briefly. Nothing here gives EMG a role in measuring fat loss. It must not be presented as fat loss, because sections 3–4 show the two are not the same.
