# W5 — Exercise strategy for losing fat while keeping muscle (trained lifter)

Agent W5, 2026-11-04. Population: resistance-trained men, intermediate to advanced (Kye).
Access caveat up front: **no full text was read.** PMC was blocked from this session (WebFetch: ENOTFOUND), so every
number below comes from an abstract quoted in a search result (ABSTRACT) or from a review/press/blog (SECONDARY).
Rows, DOIs and access flags are in `W5-studies.csv`; every search is in `W5-searchlog.md`.

## Ten-line summary (plain English)

1. **Keep lifting heavy and progressively. It is the single thing that most protects muscle in a deficit.** Diet +
   resistance training keeps lean mass where diet alone or diet + cardio loses it (Hunter 2008, Geliebter 1997). Strong.
2. **You don't need more sets to keep muscle while cutting.** In trained men, 3 sets did as well as 5 (Roth 2023).
   Keep the load and the effort. Moderate, but the study is small.
3. **Kye's 200–300 kcal/day deficit is safe but probably too slow to measure.** In one small study, a 300 kcal/day
   deficit produced *no* detectable fat loss in 4 weeks, while 750 kcal/day did, with no loss of fat-free mass
   (Huovinen 2015). The evidence-backed range is **about 0.5–0.7% of body weight per week**: for an 85 kg lifter,
   roughly 0.4–0.6 kg/week, or a deficit of about 450–650 kcal/day (Garthe 2011, Helms 2014). Larger deficits blunt
   muscle gain (Murphy & Koehler 2022, about −500 kcal/day). Moderate.
4. **Cardio helps fat loss mainly by burning energy. Its type barely matters.** HIIT and steady cardio give the same
   fat loss (Steele 2021, rated high certainty; Wewege 2017; Keating 2017). The 2019 paper claiming HIIT is "28.5%
   better" was **retracted**. Strong.
5. **Cardio costs little muscle if you keep it sensible.** Whole-muscle growth is not compromised on average
   (Schumann 2022). Any cost is small, goes up with dose, and is worse with running than cycling (Wilson 2012;
   Lundberg 2022). Prefer cycling, incline walking or rowing, and keep hard running low. Moderate.
6. **If cardio and lifting share a day, lift first, or split them by 3 hours or more.** This protects lower-body
   strength and power. It makes no difference to fat loss or muscle size (Eddens 2018; Schumann 2022). Moderate.
7. **Exercise burns fewer calories than the watch says.** On average about 28% of added activity energy is offset by
   a lower basal burn (Careau 2021). Total expenditure plateaus at high activity (Pontzer 2016), and people tend to
   eat some of it back (Thomas 2012). Set your intake from your weekly weigh-in trend, not from calories burned in
   exercise. Moderate.
8. **Daily steps are the cheapest extra energy burn, and the first thing to sink without you noticing when dieting.**
   This is a plausible mechanism, but I found no direct step-count data in dieting lifters. Treat a fixed daily step
   target as a sensible guard, not a proven intervention. Limited.
9. **Sleep is part of the plan.** In 10 dieters, 5.5 h instead of 8.5 h of sleep cut fat loss by 55% and raised
   fat-free-mass loss by 60% over 2 weeks (Nedeltcheva 2010). The study is tiny and short, but the effect is large and
   points one way. Limited to moderate.
10. **"Recomp" (losing fat and gaining muscle at once) does happen in trained lifters, but slowly and mostly in people
    with fat to lose or a training change.** For an intermediate or advanced lifter, the realistic aim in a cut is to
    keep muscle and strength, not to build them (Barakat 2020; Roth 2023 lost lean mass on 2.8 g/kg protein). Limited
    to moderate.

**Kye's model, as far as exercise is concerned:**
- "A 200–300 kcal/day deficit" is **partly right.** The direction is right and it is gentle on muscle, but it is
  slower than it needs to be, and too small to see on a scale through water and glycogen noise.
- "Burn glycogen first, then fat, so do fasted cardio and stay flat" is **wrong as a fat-loss strategy.** Over weeks,
  fat mass follows energy balance, not the fuel burned in the session. The cleanest test is that hard interval
  training (mostly glycogen) and easy steady cardio (more fat) give the same fat loss (Steele 2021). Fasted cardio and
  carb cycling are covered in detail by the nutrition agents.
- Spot reduction and arm fat are covered by the regional-fat agent. All I add is one point: **visceral (deep belly)
  fat** responds to exercise even without diet (Vissers 2013), most clearly to aerobic work (Ismail 2012). That is a
  difference between fat depots, not proof that you can train fat off one limb.

## 1. Resistance training preserves lean mass in a deficit

| Evidence | What it shows | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Hunter 2008, RCT, overweight women dieted to BMI <25 (~12 kg loss) | FFM: RT 46.9→47.2 kg (held); aerobic 45.4→44.4; no exercise 47.9→46.4. REE and strength held only with RT | moderate (untrained women) |
| Geliebter 1997, RCT, obese, 70%-of-RMR formula diet, 8 wk | Same ~9 kg weight loss in every arm. The strength arm lost significantly less FFM. RMR fell in all arms equally, so lifting did **not** stop metabolic slowdown | moderate |
| Weiss 2017, RCT, n = 52, matched ~7% loss | Diet alone: −2% whole-body and −4% leg lean mass. Diet + endurance exercise: about half that. Exercise only: no loss | moderate |
| Cava 2017 (review) | Both endurance and resistance exercise help preserve muscle. Protein preserves mass but not strength | review |
| Roth 2023, RCT, 38 **trained** men, 30 kcal/kg, 2.8 g/kg FFM protein | 3 vs 5 sets/exercise: same lean-mass and rectus femoris thickness outcome. Both groups lost some lean mass | limited–moderate (small, short) |

**Mechanism:** mechanical tension tells the body to keep muscle. Without that signal, a deficit is met partly by
breaking down muscle protein. Note that most of these trials are in untrained, overweight people, who respond more
strongly than Kye will. The trained-men trial (Roth) is the most applicable, and it shows that **lean mass still
drifts down** in a deficit despite high protein. Some of that "lean" is glycogen and water, which DXA and ultrasound
read as tissue.

**Practical:** keep the main lifts heavy (about 3–8 RM work in the program), stay at about 0–3 reps in reserve on
working sets, and keep weekly sets near the volume used to build the muscle. Cutting volume by about 40% did not cost
muscle (Roth). Cutting load or effort is the thing to avoid. This is an inference from the broader literature, not
directly tested in a deficit.

## 2. Rate of loss and deficit size

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| Garthe 2011, RCT, 24 elite athletes, 4 RT sessions/wk | 0.7%/wk: lean body mass **+2.1%** (±0.4). 1.4%/wk: −0.2% (±0.7). Between-group p<.01. Confound: the slow group trained about 3 weeks longer. **No difference at 12-month follow-up.** |
| Mero 2010, 15 women | 0.5 kg/wk (550 kcal/day) favoured over 1 kg/wk (1100 kcal/day), with the faster arm looking more catabolic. (This is a women's study. The brief's "Mero 2010" sprinter study is actually Huovinen 2015.) |
| Huovinen 2015, 15 male jumpers and sprinters, 4 wk | **750 kcal/day:** −2.2 kg body mass and −1.7 kg fat, FFM and testosterone unchanged, jump +2.6 cm. **300 kcal/day: no significant change in anything.** |
| Helms 2014 (bodybuilding review) | Lose 0.5–1% of body weight per week to maximise muscle retention |
| Murphy & Koehler 2022, 59 studies, 1495 people | A deficit impairs RT lean gains (ES −0.57) but not strength (ES −0.31, NS). Lean gain falls to about zero near a ~500 kcal/day deficit. That threshold is a regression estimate, and a 2025 critique says it is over-read |

**Verdict on 200–300 kcal/day:** it is not harmful, and it is the right *kind* of number. But 250 kcal/day is about
0.2–0.25 kg of fat per week, below what a scale can resolve week to week against 1–2 kg of water and glycogen swing,
and below the 0.5–0.7%/week band where the athlete data live. The one trial that tested about 300 kcal/day
(Huovinen) found nothing measurable in 4 weeks. A **trained lifter can usually run about 0.5–0.7% of body weight per
week** (about 450–650 kcal/day for 85 kg) without losing strength. Go slower (about 0.5%) when leaner, and when
strength on the main lifts starts falling. Strength falling across 2–3 sessions is the practical early warning; this
is coaching inference, not a trial result.

## 3. Aerobic vs resistance vs combined

- **Willis 2012 (STRRIDE AT/RT, 119 completers, 8 months, no diet):** aerobic training lost more body mass and fat
  than resistance training. Combined lost the same fat as aerobic alone at twice the time, and was the only option
  that also added lean mass. Caveats: the arms were not energy-matched, the subjects were untrained, and dropout was
  high.
- **Reading for Kye:** lifting is the muscle-keeper. Cardio is an energy-burner that can replace eating less. Fat loss
  comes from the total deficit; the choice of mode is about what it does to muscle, recovery and adherence.

## 4. HIIT vs steady cardio

| Meta-analysis | Result |
|---|---|
| Wewege 2017 (13 trials) | No difference in fat mass or waist. HIIT ~40% less time. About 2 kg fat over ~10 wk |
| Keating 2017 (31 studies) | No difference. Low-volume protocols trended towards favouring steady cardio (p = 0.09). Neither clinically meaningful in the short term |
| Viana 2019 | "28.5% more fat loss with intervals". **RETRACTED.** Do not use |
| Steele 2021 | Fat mass SMD −0.016 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.04), **GRADE high**. Intensity pattern has minimal influence on fat or FFM |

**Why it matters for Kye's glycogen model:** HIIT runs mostly on glycogen and steady cardio burns relatively more fat
during the session, yet fat loss over weeks is the same. What is burned in the session (acute substrate use) does not
decide long-term fat-mass change; the total energy balance does. For a lifter, HIIT also adds recovery cost and some
interference (next section). **Low-to-moderate steady cardio is the default.** Use one short interval session a week
only if time is the binding constraint.

## 5. Interference: does cardio cost muscle?

- **Wilson 2012** (21 studies): hypertrophy ES 1.23 for strength only vs 0.85 for concurrent. Running, not cycling,
  impaired hypertrophy and strength. The penalty grows with cardio frequency and duration.
- **Schumann 2022** (43 studies): **no compromise** of whole-muscle hypertrophy or max strength, independent of
  modality, frequency or training status. Explosive strength was hurt only when both were done in the same session,
  not when they were 3 h or more apart.
- **Lundberg 2022** (15 studies, same protocol as Schumann so not independent): small fibre-level penalty, SMD −0.23
  (p = 0.050), driven by type I fibres and running.

**Plan:** cycling, incline treadmill walking or rowing over running. Moderate dose, about 2–4 × 20–40 min/week. Keep
cardio off the legs before leg days where possible. Strength is the thing to protect; size is more robust.

## 6. Cardio timing relative to lifting

- **Eddens 2018** (10 studies): lifting first gave +6.91% lower-body dynamic strength (95% CI 1.96–11.87). There was
  no effect on hypertrophy, VO2max or body fat. A second 2018 meta-analysis (J Sports Sci 36:1212; authors not
  verified) agreed.
- **Schumann 2022:** separating sessions by 3 h or more removes the explosive-strength penalty.
- **Practical:** lift first, or do cardio in a separate session (for example morning walk, evening lift). Timing has no
  effect on fat loss.

## 7. Steps, NEAT and exercise compensation

- **Pontzer 2016** (n = 332, doubly labelled water): total expenditure rises with activity at the low end and
  **plateaus** at high activity.
- **Careau 2021** (n = 1,754): on average **28%** of activity energy is offset by a lower basal burn, and more in
  people with more fat.
- **Thomas 2012:** exercise trials lose less weight than predicted, mainly because the doses are small and intake
  rises.
- **Isola 2023** (physique athletes, press release only): resting energy expenditure fell more than tissue loss
  predicts during prep, along with T3 and resting heart rate. It recovered within about 5 months.
- **Gap:** no direct data found on step-count decline in dieting lifters (1 search).

**Practical:** don't "eat back" logged exercise calories. Keep a fixed daily step floor (for example 8–10k; a
pragmatic choice, not a tested dose) so that walking cannot quietly drift down. Adjust intake every 2–3 weeks from
the averaged scale trend. The averaged scale trend already includes all compensation, which is why it beats any
calorie-burn estimate.

## 8. Body recomposition in trained lifters

- **Barakat 2020** (review, not read in full): recomposition is documented in trained people given progressive RT,
  high protein, modest energy status and sleep. It warns that DXA and BIA "lean" includes water and glycogen.
- **Longland 2016:** a ~40% deficit plus RT and HIIT 6 days/week. 2.4 vs 1.2 g/kg protein gave more fat loss (−4.8 vs
  −3.5 kg) and, per the title and lead author, more lean gain (+1.2 vs +0.1 kg). But the men were not trained lifters,
  the training was new to them, and it lasted 4 weeks.
- **Garthe 2011 (slow arm):** +2.1% lean mass in elite athletes losing 0.7%/week.
- **Roth 2023:** trained men lost lean mass in a moderate deficit despite 2.8 g/kg FFM protein.

**Honest verdict:** for an intermediate or advanced lifter, recomp is real but slow and unreliable to measure. Most
"recomp" results come from people who were newer to training, fatter, changing program, or measured by DXA/BIA within
its error. **Plan to keep muscle in the cut and build it in a later surplus.** Treat any recomp as a bonus.

## 9. Visceral fat responds to exercise without weight loss

- **Vissers 2013** (15 studies, 852 people, no caloric restriction): visceral fat g = −0.50 (95% CI −0.66 to −0.34).
  Moderate-to-high-intensity aerobic work was best, with reductions over 40 cm² possible in men in 12 weeks.
- **Ismail 2012** (35 RCTs): aerobic vs control ES −0.33 (−0.52 to −0.14). Resistance training vs control was not
  significant, with high heterogeneity, and a later analysis suggests RT does work without a diet.

**Relevance:** visceral fat is metabolically "hungrier" and goes first with exercise, so the waist can shrink before
the scale moves. This is about a fat *depot*, not about choosing a limb. It does not support spot reduction (see the
regional-fat agent).

## 10. Sleep and stress in a deficit

**Nedeltcheva 2010** (crossover, n = 10, 14 days each): with 5.5 h vs 8.5 h of sleep on the same diet, fat loss was
0.6 vs 1.4 kg and FFM loss 2.4 vs 1.5 kg. There was more hunger and less fat oxidation. The study is tiny and short,
the subjects were not lifters, and FFM includes water. But the direction is unambiguous and it is a metabolic-ward
style design. **Protect 7–9 h.** Stress was not directly studied here. The cortisol association in Longland 2016 is
correlational only.

## 11. GLP-1 drugs (context only)

- **Share of weight lost as lean mass** (2026 meta-analysis of RCTs, Diabetes Obes Metab, PMID 41877354):
  semaglutide 35.2% (31.5–38.9), tirzepatide 25.4% (22.8–28.0), liraglutide 26.8%. **Lifestyle alone 26.2%, the same
  as the drugs. Lifestyle + RT 17.5% (14.2–20.8).**
- **STEP 1 DXA** (industry-funded): lean mass about 39% of loss. **SURMOUNT-1 DXA:** about 26%.
- **Lundgren 2021 NEJM:** liraglutide + exercise cut body-fat % about twice as much as either alone (−3.9 vs −1.7
  and −1.9 points).
- **Take-home:** the drugs do not uniquely waste muscle; any large deficit does. Resistance training and protein
  reduce the lean share. The RT comparison is across trials, not randomised within a drug trial, and DXA lean mass is
  not the same as muscle. Not relevant to Kye's plan unless prescribed.

## Evidence-ranked plan for a trained lifter cutting fat

| Rank | Action | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Keep progressive, heavy resistance training.** Same exercises and loads; 0–3 reps in reserve; volume can drop to about 60% without loss | strong (untrained), moderate (trained) |
| 2 | **Deficit of about 0.5–0.7% body weight/week** (~450–650 kcal/day at 85 kg), slower when lean or when strength drops. 200–300 kcal/day is acceptable but slow and hard to verify | moderate |
| 3 | **Protein high** (about 1.6–2.4 g/kg; Helms suggests 2.3–3.1 g/kg lean mass when lean). Owned by the nutrition agents | moderate |
| 4 | **Sleep 7–9 h** | limited–moderate (large effect, tiny study) |
| 5 | **Steady, low-impact cardio** (cycling, incline walking, rowing) 2–4 × 20–40 min, mainly as a lever on the deficit. HIIT optional, at most once a week | strong (HIIT = MICT for fat), moderate (interference) |
| 6 | **Lift first, or separate cardio by 3 h or more** | moderate (strength only) |
| 7 | **Daily step floor; never eat back exercise calories; adjust from the 2–3 week scale trend** | moderate (compensation), limited (steps) |
| 8 | **Track strength on 2–3 main lifts as the muscle-loss alarm.** Body-fat scales and BIA are too noisy for 0.2 kg/week changes | inference |
| 9 | **Expect to keep, not build.** Treat recomp as a bonus | limited–moderate |

**Ignore:** fasted cardio for extra fat loss, the "fat-burning zone", HIIT as a fat-loss multiplier (its supporting
meta-analysis was retracted), and targeting one limb.

**Product note** (speculative, no evidence): the band's per-set fatigue and rep-in-reserve signal could serve as the
"effort held" check in rank 1, by showing whether sets in a cut still reach the same proximity to failure. That idea
is untested.

## Flags

- Most of the evidence base is untrained or overweight subjects. Only Roth 2023, Garthe 2011 and Huovinen 2015 are
  trained or athletic, and all three are small (15–38 people).
- Viana 2019 is retracted. Wilson 2012 effect sizes and Barakat 2020 conclusions are SECONDARY.
- The GLP-1 trials (STEP, SURMOUNT, and probably Lundgren) carry manufacturer funding.
- Murphy & Koehler's ~500 kcal threshold is a regression estimate under critique.

  cardio (memory pointer only); Ribeiro 2022 and Neeland 2024 (not found or not searched).
