# F2 — Training volume (weekly hard sets per muscle) and frequency, in trained lifters

Agent F2, 2026-11-02.

## Ten-line summary

1. **Volume is a real lever, and a top-2 one.** There are six meta-analyses (Krieger 2010, Schoenfeld 2017, Baz-Valle 2021 and 2022, Pelland 2026, Remmert 2025 preprint). Every one finds that more hard sets give more growth on average. **But the pooled studies overlap heavily**, so this is closer to one evidence base analysed six times than to six independent confirmations. (I, moderate)
2. **The best current model is Pelland 2026** (Sports Med 56:481–505, DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w, which I **confirmed this session** against a ResearchGate copy of the published PDF). It pools 67 studies (2,058 participants, a figure from blogs only). Hypertrophy keeps rising with weekly sets but with **diminishing returns and widening uncertainty**, and strength flattens sooner. I could not reach the marginal gain per set; it is in the paper or on OSF and has not been verified.
3. **Counting a set that trains a muscle only indirectly as 0.5 of a set fits the data best** (Pelland 2026). So a bench set is about half a triceps set. That matters for a one-muscle band.
4. **The dose-response is steepest at low volume.** Schoenfeld 2017 reports about 5.4%, 6.6% and 9.8% growth for <5, 5–9 and 10+ sets a week, from the author's blog, with a category test of P = 0.074. Krieger 2010 reports one set per exercise at ES 0.24, 2–3 sets at 0.34 and 4–6 sets at 0.44.
5. **In trained lifters above about 12–20 sets a week, the trials disagree.**
   - Schoenfeld 2019 (up to 5 sets per exercise) and Brigatto 2022 (32 > 16 sets) favour more.
   - Heaselgrave 2019 (27 ≈ 18), Aube 2022 (24 not better than 18), Enes 2024 and Barsuhn 2025 (adding sets did not add measurable size) and Räntilä 2026 (60 ≈ 40 sets) do not.
   - Baz-Valle 2022 found 12–20 ≈ >20 sets for quads and biceps, and >20 better for triceps.
6. **Frequency is mostly a way of delivering volume.** With volume equated it has no detectable effect on hypertrophy: Schoenfeld 2019 (25 studies), Pelland 2026 (posterior 91.3% that the slope is above zero, about 0.32% per extra session, 95% CrI −0.14 to 0.82, from a secondary source), and a 2026 reanalysis preprint. Its usefulness is in spreading sets out: the Remmert 2025 preprint puts the point past which extra sets in one session make no detectable difference at about 11 fractional sets per session for hypertrophy.
7. **"Hard set" is the unit, and effort is what defines it.** Set counts predict growth only for sets taken to or near failure in about the 6–20+ rep range (Baz-Valle 2021). **Volume is therefore conditional on effort.** A set at 5+ RIR is not the set these meta-analyses counted.
8. **Why more sets give more growth (M, limited–moderate):**
   - One bout raises muscle protein synthesis for about 24–36 h in trained men (MacDougall 1995).
   - Three sets raise myofibrillar protein synthesis more than one (Burd 2010).
   - Within the same person, 3 sets beat 1, and how much better was associated with early ribosome biogenesis (Hammarström 2020, untrained).
   - Growth tracks repeated protein-synthesis elevations once muscle damage has subsided (Damas 2016).
9. **Why returns diminish (M/H, limited):**
   - Protein synthesis saturates within a session.
   - Damage and fatigue cost recovery capacity.
   - At extreme volume, measured "growth" may be part fluid or sarcoplasmic: Haun 2019 found about −30% actin/myosin concentration in a ~23% larger fibre (one uncontrolled study), and ultrasound measures of high-volume groups carry an edema risk.
   - The men's "ceiling" trial by Barbalho is **retracted** and must not be used.
10. **Individual response is large and mostly unexplained.** Within-subject, only about 13 of 34 people grew meaningfully more on 3 sets than 1 (Hammarström). Frequency responses split roughly evenly (Damas 2019). The "non-responder" label is partly measurement noise. No study shows that a device can predict who needs more sets.

## Scorecard on the five ranking criteria

| criterion | score | basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Effect size at realistic contrasts | **Medium–large going from about 4 to about 10–12 sets a week; small and uncertain above about 20** | Schoenfeld 2017: ~5% vs ~10% gain. Krieger: ES 0.24 → 0.44. In trained lifters, 16 vs 32 sets is significant in some trials and absent in others. |
| 2. Certainty | **Moderate** | 6 meta-analyses with heavy overlap. Most primary trials run 6–12 weeks, use ultrasound, have small n and are unblinded. One retraction sits in the field. Heterogeneity is wide at the high end. |
| 3. Evidence in trained lifters | **Moderate**, better than most factors | About 10 trials in trained men (Ostrowski 1997, Schoenfeld 2019, Heaselgrave 2019, Brigatto 2022, Aube 2022, Haun 2018/2019, Enes 2024, Barsuhn 2025, Räntilä 2026). Almost all are men, quadriceps or arms, and ≤12 weeks. Women are nearly absent. |
| 4. Controllable set to set | **High** | A lifter decides every set. Effort decides whether a set counts. |
| 5. Dose-response shape | **Concave: no clear plateau in the meta-regression, but a practical flattening in trained trials somewhere around 12–24 hard sets a week, with a per-session ceiling around 6–11 sets** | Pelland and Remmert. No harm zone for hypertrophy is shown, though strength and performance may suffer (Räntilä deadlift p = 0.043; Ostrowski's T:C ratio trend). |

**Where volume ranks against effort.** Effort is the gate. A set far from failure barely counts, so with no effort, volume does not exist in the sense these trials used. Once sets are hard, the *range* of outcome that volume controls (roughly 2× growth from 4 to 12+ sets a week) is larger than the range proximity to failure controls within 0–4 RIR. In trained lifters, failure vs non-failure is ES 0.15 (0.03–0.26) (Grgic 2022, via A6). **I rank volume #2 behind effort, and as co-#1 in practical terms for a lifter already training hard.** The certainty is about the same as effort's, and volume is easier for a device to count than RIR is to estimate.

## 1. Evidence: meta-analyses

These overlap; there is no independent replication across them.

| meta | k | trained? | finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Krieger 2010 JSCR | 8 studies, 55 ES | mixed | multiple > single sets, ES difference 0.10 (0.02–0.19). Trend 0.24 / 0.34 / 0.44 for 1 / 2–3 / 4–6 sets. 2–3 vs 4–6 sets p = 0.29. |
| Schoenfeld 2017 J Sports Sci | 15 studies, 34 groups | mostly untrained | graded; 10+ sets about 2× <5 sets; category test P = 0.074. The often-quoted "0.37% per set" is from memory and **UNVERIFIED**. |
| Baz-Valle 2021 JSCR | 14 studies | trained, at least 1 year | set count is a valid dose for sets to or near failure at 6–20+ reps. |
| Baz-Valle 2022 J Hum Kinet | 7 RCTs (6 pooled) | trained | 12–20 vs >20 sets a week: quads p = 0.19, biceps p = 0.59, triceps p = 0.01 favouring >20. |
| Pelland 2026 Sports Med | 67 studies (2,058 participants, from blogs); hypertrophy subset reportedly 35 studies / 220 effects | mixed, with training status as a moderator | concave rise for hypertrophy; strength flattens sooner; fractional counting fits best; frequency affects strength only. |
| Remmert 2025 SportRxiv preprint | the same dataset | mixed | per-session volume: point past which extra sets make no detectable difference ≈ 11 fractional sets for hypertrophy and ≈ 2 direct sets for strength. |
| Schoenfeld 2016 Sports Med (frequency) | 10 studies | mixed | 2×/week beat 1×: ES 0.49 vs 0.30 (p = 0.002). **Mostly not volume-equated.** |
| Schoenfeld 2019 J Sports Sci (frequency) | 25 studies | mixed | volume-equated: no effect. Not equated: higher frequency helps. |
| Grgic 2018 Sports Med (frequency, strength); Ralston 2018 SMO; Grgic 2019 JSAMS review | k not verified | mixed | volume-equated frequency: no strength effect (p = 0.421 in Grgic). |

**Overlap.**
- Pelland 2026 almost certainly contains every trial in Krieger, Schoenfeld 2017 and Baz-Valle 2022.
- Remmert shares Pelland's dataset.
- The 2026 reanalysis is Pelland's own data.
- Counting honestly: about **70 unique primary studies** sit behind the volume claim, of which **roughly 10–15 are in trained lifters with set-count contrasts.** "Hundreds" is not defensible.

## 2. Evidence: primary trials in trained lifters

| study | contrast (sets/muscle/week) | n | weeks | hypertrophy result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ostrowski 1997 | 3 / 6 / 12 per muscle group (triceps ≈ 7/14/28 including indirect) | 27 | 10 | similar |
| Schoenfeld 2019 MSSE | 1 / 3 / 5 sets per exercise, 3×/week | 34 | 8 | graded, 5 sets best; strength similar |
| Heaselgrave 2019 | 9 (1×/week) / 18 / 27 (2×/week) biceps | 49 | 6 | ≈ 4.3 / 9.5 / 5.4% (secondary); 27 not better than 18. **Frequency confounded.** |
| Brigatto 2022 | 16 / 24 / 32 | trained men | 8 | 32 > 16 lower body; 24 in between; 1-minute rests |
| Aube 2022 | 12 / 18 / 24 quads | 35 | — | secondary sources **conflict** on hypertrophy; 18 sets best for strength |
| Haun 2018 / 2019 | ramp from 10 to 32 sets a week | ~30 | 6 | gains, but much of it fluid or sarcoplasmic; no control group |
| Enes 2024 MSSE | constant 22 vs +4 or +6 sets per fortnight | 31 | 12 | strength dose-response; size p = 0.067–0.076 (not significant) |
| Barsuhn 2025 J Appl Physiol | maintain vs +30% vs +60% of previous sets | 29 completers (55 enrolled) | 8 | maintaining was enough |

**Reading of the table.** Trained lifters show clear benefit up to roughly the volume they were already doing, plus some. Large jumps above about 20–25 sets a week show inconsistent or no extra growth, with more strain. Every null result comes from a small trial and none is a test of equivalence. "No benefit above X" is **not** established; "benefit above X is uncertain and small if present" is.

## 3. Mechanism: why it works

Evidence codes: M = human mechanistic, A = animal, I = inference from training outcomes.

- **Repeated stimulation of protein synthesis (M, moderate).** A heavy bout in trained men raises protein synthesis by +50% at 4 h and +109% at 24 h, and it is back near baseline by 36 h (MacDougall 1995; n ≈ 6, mixed protein). The trained-state response is shorter than the untrained one. Hypertrophy is the sum of many of these windows.
  - More sets per bout give a larger response: in Burd 2010 (n = 8), 3 sets raised myofibrillar protein synthesis more than 1. This is an acute measure and is not evidence of long-term growth on its own.
  - More bouts give more windows. That is why frequency helps when it adds volume and not when volume is fixed.
- **Translational capacity (M, limited).** In Hammarström 2020, the extra benefit of 3 sets over 1 was associated with early total-RNA (ribosome) accumulation. The study was in untrained people and is associative.
- **Damage versus growth (M, moderate).** In Damas 2016, myofibrillar protein synthesis correlated with hypertrophy only after damage subsided (weeks 3 and 10, untrained). Damas 2018 argues that damage does not drive growth. Volume adds stimulus until it mainly adds damage and repair.
- **Accumulated tension exposure (I).** Each hard set gives the fibres a few high-tension, near-failure reps. Volume multiplies those exposures, which is why effort and volume are inseparable.

**Why returns diminish (H/M, limited).**
1. Protein synthesis within one session saturates. The Remmert per-session point (≈ 11 fractional sets) is the outcome-level analogue.
2. Fatigue and damage accumulate, and recovery capacity is finite. Supporting signs: Ostrowski's T:C trend, Enes's strain scores, and Räntilä's lower deadlift.
3. Measurement: high-volume "growth" can be edema or sarcoplasmic expansion (Haun 2018 ECW critique; Haun 2019). Alvarez (n = 13) suggests swelling resolves within 24 h, which partly answers this, and the question remains open for multi-week accumulation.
4. Trained muscle is already near its ceiling, so each extra set buys less.

## 4. Specifics for trained lifters

- **The effective dose depends on recent history.** Scarpelli 2022 and Barsuhn 2025 point to the useful increment being relative to what the lifter already does, not an absolute number. For a band this suggests logging each user's own baseline volume per muscle.
- **Sets in the 6–20+ rep range, near failure, are the countable unit** (Baz-Valle 2021). Very heavy 1–5 rep sets under-predict growth from set counts.
- **Indirect sets count as about half** (Pelland 2026). One band on the triceps sees triceps EMG during a bench press. Whether its EMG can tell a "fractional" set from a "direct" one is H.
- **Frequency of 2 or more sessions per muscle per week** is a practical way to keep per-session volume under the ≈ 11-set ceiling. It is not an independent hypertrophy lever.

## 6. Individual response and "non-responders"

- **Hammarström 2020** (untrained, within-subject): on average 3 sets > 1. About 38% (13/34) showed meaningfully more hypertrophy with 3 sets.
- **Damas 2019** (within-subject frequency, training status unverified): an even split between favouring high frequency, favouring low frequency and no difference.
- **Ahtiainen 2016** (n = 287, untrained): 29% were low responders for size when compared against the control group's upper 95% CI. Part of that is measurement error.
- **Räntilä 2026:** extra volume did not convert non-responders.

The honest conclusion: some people probably benefit more from volume than others. No trial has shown a test, physiological or sensor-based, that identifies them prospectively. Any "your optimal volume" feature would be H, and the n-of-1 noise would be large (ultrasound typical error versus 1–3% effects).

## 7. Open disputes

1. **Where the trained curve flattens (12, 20, 30 or 40 sets?).** Pelland's model has no clear plateau, and individual trials do. That is consistent with a shallow curve plus noise, and with heterogeneity in how sets were counted, how close to failure they were, and how long rests were (Brigatto used 1-minute rests).
2. **Whether high-volume gains are contractile.** See Haun 2019 and the edema question.
3. **Whether volume or frequency drives strength.** Pelland finds a frequency effect on strength; the volume-equated meta-analyses do not.
4. **What counts as a "hard set".** Blog definitions run from 0–3 RIR to 0–4 RIR. No meta-analysis has a measured-RIR cut-off; Robinson 2024 used *estimated* RIR (A1).
5. **Fractional counting is a fitted modelling choice, not measured muscle-level stimulus.**

## 8. What this means for TENSR (H unless stated)

- **Counting hard sets for the instrumented muscle is feasible and is the best-evidenced dose metric a single band can log.** Rep bursts plus a set boundary give a set count.
- **The hard part is "hard".** Whether a set qualifies depends on proximity to failure, which is the RIR problem (A1/A2). Following A6, use user-tagged effort or RPE as the qualifier until an EMG-based estimate is validated.
- **Fractional sets.** A band on one muscle cannot see synergists. A label-based fractional tally per exercise is needed for any "weekly sets per muscle" view across muscles. Whether EMG amplitude could separate direct from indirect loading of the instrumented muscle is untested.
- **Do not display an "optimal weekly sets" target or a "stimulus score".** The literature supports a curve with diminishing returns and wide individual spread, not a number. A defensible display is "hard sets this week vs your 4-week average", consistent with Scarpelli and Barsuhn: progress relative to your own baseline.
- **Per-session flag.** A soft note when one session exceeds about 10 hard sets for the instrumented muscle is consistent with the Remmert preprint, but it rests on one preprint. Keep it optional.
- **Frequency is logging, not sensing.** It is trivially derivable from session timestamps.
