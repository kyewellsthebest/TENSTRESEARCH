# F1 — Effort / proximity to failure in trained lifters

Agent F1, 2026-11-02. 30 WebSearches. Every fetch route was blocked: PMC, sportrxiv, AUT repository, doi.org, Crossref, PubMed and Europe PMC. **No full text was read.** Each claim below is abstract-snippet level (ABSTRACT), secondary (SECONDARY), or carried from A6's earlier audit (A6). The study list is in `F1-studies.csv` (50 rows) and the search trail in `F1-searchlog.md`.

Evidence codes: **I** = longitudinal training outcome, **A** = acute physiology, **M** = mechanism, **H** = hypothesis. Strengths: strong / moderate / limited / very limited.

## 10-line summary

1. **Effort is a gate, not a dial.** Sets must get reasonably close to failure to count. Inside the range trained lifters actually use (0 to about 3 RIR), the extra benefit of going all the way to failure is **small to trivial**. (I, moderate)
2. Refalo 2023 (15 studies): failure vs non-failure ES **0.19 (0.00–0.37)**. When failure is defined strictly as momentary failure, ES is **0.12 (−0.13 to 0.37)**. Grgic 2022 (15 studies): pooled ES 0.22 (−0.11 to 0.55); trained subgroup ES 0.15 (0.03–0.26), but that subgroup rests on about 2 studies. (I, moderate)
3. Robinson 2024 treated proximity as a continuous variable (estimated RIR, about 55 studies per secondary sources). Hypertrophy rises as sets end closer to failure, strength barely changes, and **heavier loads make proximity matter less**. The authors call it exploratory, and the RIR values were estimated after the fact. (I, limited–moderate)
4. **Direct trials in trained lifters show no meaningful growth benefit from failure over 1–2 RIR:**
   - Refalo 2024: quadriceps +0.18 cm in both arms.
   - Santanielo 2020: CSA +13.5% to failure vs +18.1% non-failure, no significant difference.
   - Martikainen 2025: constant 1 RIR vs progressive 4→1 RIR, no difference.

   The exception is a single set per exercise: Hermann 2025 (n = 42) found a modest trend favouring failure. (I, moderate)
5. **Load moderates the effect.** Failure mattered at 30% 1RM but not at 80% 1RM (Lasevicius 2022, untrained). This is the mirror of Robinson's moderator. Light, high-rep sets are where proximity matters most. (I, limited–moderate)
6. **Velocity loss is a continuous effort proxy with a shallow slope** (Jukic 2023: 18 acute and 19 longitudinal studies). Higher velocity-loss thresholds give slightly more hypertrophy, b = 0.006 per percentage point of velocity loss (0.001–0.012). They do not change strength and they cost jump and sprint performance. Pareja-Blanco 2017 and 2020: about 20% velocity loss captured most of the growth, and 40% added fatigue and loss of type IIX fibres. In these trials higher velocity loss also means more reps, so the result is confounded with volume. (I, moderate)
7. **Failure costs more:**
   - more acute neuromuscular fatigue (FAIL > 1-RIR > 3-RIR, Refalo 2023 SMOA)
   - more velocity and repetition loss (Refalo 2024)
   - more discomfort (Refalo 2025)
   - non-failure is better for strength when volume is not equated (Grgic 2022)

   (A/I, moderate)
8. **Why effort matters (M).** Recruitment follows the size principle. As fatigue sets in, higher-threshold units are recruited and contraction velocity falls, which per the force–velocity relation raises the tension on each fibre. With lighter loads that state is reached only near failure (Morton 2019; Muddle 2018). The "effective reps" model, that roughly the last 5 reps carry the stimulus, is a **hypothesis** from grey literature (Beardsley, building on Fagerli). It has never been directly tested, and the large gaps it predicts mostly do not appear. (M moderate; the effective-reps model is H)
9. **Lifters are biased RIR estimators** (Halperin 2022: 12 studies, 414 participants). They underpredict by 0.95 reps (0.17–1.73), with a between-person SD of about 1.45 and I² = 97.9%. Accuracy is worst far from failure, with light loads and with sets over 12 reps. That is exactly where proximity matters most for growth. (I/D, moderate)
10. **Is effort #1 for trained lifters?** Only in the sense of a **necessary condition**. Comparing sets that are effortful against sets that are not, it is probably the largest single factor. At the contrast people actually argue about, failure vs 1–3 RIR, its effect is smaller than the volume dose-response and roughly comparable to other second-order factors. I would rank it **#1 as a threshold and below volume as a marginal lever**. (judgement)

## 3. Mechanism — why proximity to failure matters

- **Size principle (M, strong as physiology).** Motor units are recruited small to large (Henneman 1957, cited from memory, not re-checked here). High-threshold units drive most of the fibre-level hypertrophy signal. They are recruited by high force demand *or* by fatigue of the lower-threshold units.
- **Low loads reach high-threshold units only near failure (A, moderate).**
  - Muddle 2018 (motor-unit decomposition): both 30% and 70% MVIC contractions to failure recruited additional higher-threshold units. The low-torque condition recruited smaller units throughout, and full recruitment at low torque was **subject-dependent**.
  - Morton 2019: type I and type II glycogen depletion did not depend on load when sets ended at task failure.
  - This is why effort equalises load (Schoenfeld 2017a, Morton 2016). It also explains why proximity matters more at light loads (Lasevicius 2022, Robinson 2024).
- **Heavy loads recruit most units from rep 1 (M, moderate).** The final reps add less new recruitment. Further force comes from rate coding: firing-rate increases matter more once recruitment is complete, and the recruitment ceiling is muscle-dependent (textbook; not re-searched).
  - Consequence: a set at 80% 1RM stopped at 3 RIR is already near-maximally recruited. That is consistent with Sampson 2016 and Lasevicius 2022 at 80%.
- **Force–velocity (M, moderate as physiology; H as the causal account of growth).** As fatigue slows a rep, fewer cross-bridges cycle per unit time and each attached fibre carries more force. Slow, fatigued reps therefore put high tension on the recruited high-threshold fibres. This is the mechanistic core of the "effective reps" idea. Velocity loss is its observable signature.
- **"Effective reps" (H).**
  - Origin: grey literature, chiefly Chris Beardsley (c. 2018–2019), who credits Børge Fagerli's myo-reps reasoning. It is not a peer-reviewed model, and the "~5 reps" number is not derived from any measurement.
  - Tests are indirect only:
    - **Consistent:** low and high loads to failure give similar growth; failure matters at light load and not heavy (Lasevicius 2022); the Robinson 2024 load moderator; velocity-loss studies.
    - **Strained:** the model predicts big losses at 3–5 RIR on light/moderate loads and that "junk" early reps add nothing. Volume-equated non-failure trials (Lacerda 2020, Santanielo 2020) and Martikainen's 4-RIR weeks show no such losses. Critics say the same: Nuckols at Stronger By Science, and Henselmans.
  - Status: **plausible, not established.**
- **Fatigue is not the stimulus (I, moderate).** More velocity loss and metabolic fatigue at fixed endpoint effort do not add growth (Refalo 2024; A1's cluster/drop-set evidence). Fatigue is a by-product and a cost.
- **sEMG amplitude is not the stimulus proxy (A, moderate).** In Morton 2019, sEMG amplitude did **not** track fibre glycogen depletion or signalling. Amplitude also plateaus 3–5 reps before failure (Sundstrup 2012). See also Vigotsky 2022.

## 4. RIR estimation accuracy — trained-lifter specifics

- **Halperin 2022:** −0.95-rep bias, SD ~1.45. Better near failure, with heavier loads, in later sets and in sets of ≤12 reps. Training status did not clearly moderate.
- **Helms 2017 (powerlifters, heavy):** mean absolute RPE difference 0.33 ± 0.28. Accuracy is excellent at heavy, low-rep loads.
- **FAU study (12 trained men):** bench error 1.00 ± 0.18 at 4 RIR vs 0.69 ± 0.12 at 1 RIR.
- **Zourdos 2016:** RIR-based RPE correlates with bar velocity.
- **Emanuel 2021:** each +1 RPE ≈ 11% closer to failure and −4% velocity, steeper at heavy loads.
- **Steele 2017:** accuracy improves with experience.
- **Hermann 2025, Martikainen 2025:** accuracy improves over training blocks.
- **Refalo 2024 JSCR (bench intraset RIR):** citation found, **results not retrieved**.
- **Jukic 2024:** individual RIR–velocity models reach <2 reps error (A6).
- **Implication.** Trained lifters are good near failure on heavy compounds and poor on light, long sets. The poor case is exactly the one where the hypertrophy literature says proximity matters most.

## 6. Open disputes

- **Strict failure vs 0–1 RIR.** No trial can resolve a difference this small (ES ~0.1) with n ≈ 15–40 and ultrasound. Equivalence is **not** shown, only no detectable difference.
- **Single-set / low-volume training** may be where failure matters (Hermann 2025). The effort–volume interaction is under-tested.
- **Where the curve bends.** Robinson's continuous model says it bends; the trials cluster at 0–3 RIR. Data at 4–6 RIR in trained lifters are sparse (Martikainen's early weeks only).
- **Velocity loss vs volume.** In every velocity-loss trial, higher thresholds mean more reps. Effort and volume are not separated.
- **Highly trained or elite lifters:** untested (noted by Grgic 2022 and Vieira 2021).

## 7. Note for the coordinator — what TENSR could track for this factor

These are measurement ideas, not validated features.

- **Rep count and rep-duration slowdown per set** (IMU, plus EMG burst timing). This is a limb-segment analogue of velocity loss. Pareja-Blanco and Jukic show velocity loss is the best-studied continuous effort proxy. It needs validation against bar velocity, and the band measures limb, not bar, kinematics.
- **Whether a set was "hard"** (≈ 0–3 RIR) is the outcome-relevant threshold, not "did you hit failure". A6 already flagged that a failure score rewards the costly end of the curve.
- **Load context matters.** On light, high-rep sets, an objective proximity signal would add the most value over self-report. There, humans are worst and proximity matters most. It is also where sEMG amplitude converges toward failure regardless of load (Sundstrup 2012).
- **Bar to clear.** Self-report bias ~1 rep, SD ~1.5. The best objective method so far, individual velocity models, reaches <2 reps (Jukic 2024).
